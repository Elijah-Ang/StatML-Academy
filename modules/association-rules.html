01A basket becomes a row

What exactly are we counting?

A transaction is one observed basket. An item is a product, and an itemset is a group of products. We ask how many baskets contain every item in the group. Each basket counts once, regardless of how many units of a product it contains.

A Boolean row records presence: 1 = present, 0 = absent. TID identifies the row and is never a product.

Next: A rule has two sides ↓
02A rule has two sides

What does Apple → Coke actually claim?

Apple → Coke reads: among baskets containing Apple, how often does Coke also appear? Apple is the antecedent X, and Coke is the consequent Y. The two sides must be nonempty and share no items. The joint count counts baskets containing every item from both sides.

Read the arrow as a conditional question

X = {Apple}
Y = {Coke}
X ∪ Y = {Apple, Coke}

Both Apple baskets, 100 and 300, also contain Coke. The arrow gives a direction for counting. It does not establish what happened first.

Source basis: Lecture pp.13–14. Reading receipts stay fixed when you edit the live lab.

↳
Try it

Switch from Apple → Coke to Bread + Coke → Egg. Identify the baskets that qualify for the left side before inspecting their Egg marks.

Why this works · a little deeper

A transaction satisfies X only when it contains every item in X. Bread + Coke means an AND condition, not an either/or condition.

The notation X ∪ Y combines the item names into one set. Its support counts baskets that contain all those names. In probability language, this is the intersection of the events “contains X” and “contains Y”.

Association rules describe co-occurrence. A rule by itself establishes neither a causal effect nor statistical dependence. Lift later compares the co-occurrence with an independence baseline.

Quick check · Can Apple + Coke → Coke be a valid association rule here?

No. Coke appears on both sides. The antecedent and consequent must be disjoint, otherwise the right side is partly guaranteed by the left.

The rule names the two sides. Next choose which baskets belong in its denominator.

Next: Support counts everybody ↓
03Support counts everybody

Which denominator belongs to support?

Support is the fraction of all transactions that contain the itemset. A rule’s support uses the combined set X ∪ Y: the union of the item names from both sides. Its denominator is the entire dataset, including baskets containing neither side.

All four baskets are in the denominator

count({Apple, Coke}) = 2
support(Apple → Coke) = 2/4 = 50%

Baskets 100 and 300 are the two joint matches. Baskets 200 and 400 still belong to the denominator, even though they do not contain Apple.

Source basis: Lecture pp.13–17; recurring four-basket example. Reading receipts stay fixed when you edit the live lab.

Why this works · a little deeper

Support count is an absolute number of transactions. Relative support divides that count by N, the total number of transactions. A minimum count of 2 is 50% in four baskets but 2% in one hundred baskets.

The combined itemset is unordered. Apple → Coke and Coke → Apple have the same support because they count the same co-occurrences.

Adding baskets that contain neither item leaves the numerator unchanged and enlarges the denominator. Section 15 makes this visible.

Next: Confidence zooms into X ↓
04Confidence zooms into X

Why does confidence change the denominator?

Confidence restricts attention to baskets containing X. It measures the fraction of those baskets that also contain Y. First choose the group on the left of the arrow, then count successes inside it.

A smaller group, the same joint count

confidence(Apple → Coke)
= count({Apple, Coke}) / count({Apple})
= 2/2 = 100%

All four baskets supplied the support denominator. Now keep only baskets 100 and 300, the two Apple baskets. Both also contain Coke.

Source basis: Lecture pp.15–17. Reading receipts stay fixed when you edit the live lab.

Why this works · a little deeper

In probability notation confidence is P(Y | X). The vertical bar means “given X”. It is an empirical conditional proportion in these observed transactions, not a guarantee for the next customer.

When no basket contains X, the denominator is zero and confidence is undefined. The notebook displays “Undefined” if an edit creates that case.

High confidence can arise simply because Y is common. We need the baseline support of Y before judging how surprising the rule is.

In the separate six-person lecture example, three of the four Coffee buyers also buy Milk: confidence is 3/4 = 75%. The joint count is still three, while support uses all six people: 3/6 = 50%.

Quick check · If three baskets contain X and two contain both X and Y, what is confidence?

2/3, or about 66.7%. A basket with X but without Y remains in the denominator and counts as a failure.

The left side chose the denominator. Next reverse the arrow and choose a different group.

05Reversing the arrow

What stays the same when we reverse a rule?

Reversing the arrow keeps the joint count and support unchanged. Confidence usually changes because we divide by a different group: Apple baskets in one direction and Coke baskets in the other.

Why this works · a little deeper

For disjoint X and Y, support(X → Y) = support(Y → X). Confidence(X → Y) uses count(X), while confidence(Y → X) uses count(Y).

A rule’s direction expresses the question the analyst asks. It does not encode the order of purchase. Sequential pattern mining introduces order as a separate concept.

The GPU seminar example is another useful contrast: CPU → GPU has 20% confidence, while GPU → CPU has 80%, using the same 1,200 joint purchases.

Quick check · If confidence in both directions is equal and the joint count is positive, what follows?

The antecedent and consequent counts are equal. With the same positive numerator, equal fractions require equal denominators.

A high confidence may simply reflect a common product. Next compare it with the overall baseline.

Next: Lift checks the baseline ↓
06Lift checks the baseline

Can high confidence still hide a weaker association?

Confidence describes the X group. We now compare it with how common Y is overall: the baseline is support(Y). Lift = confidence / baseline. Below 1 means Y is less common in the X group; above 1 means it is more common. At 1, the two groups have the same Y rate.

Basketball and cereal: the source counterexample

N = 5,000; Basketball = 3,000
Cereal = 3,750; Both = 2,000
confidence = 2,000/3,000 = 66.7%
baseline = 3,750/5,000 = 75%
lift = (2/3)/(3/4) = 8/9 ≈ 0.89

With independent occurrence we would expect 3,000 × 3,750 / 5,000 = 2,250 joint records. We observe only 2,000.

Source basis: Lecture p.49; seminar slides 51–52. Reading receipts stay fixed when you edit the live lab.

Why this works · a little deeper

Lift is symmetric when both itemsets have positive support: lift(X → Y) = support(X ∪ Y) / [support(X) × support(Y)]. For Apple and Coke, this is (2/4) / [(2/4) × (3/4)] = 4/3 ≈ 1.33 in either direction.

Lift above 1 indicates positive dependence relative to the marginal frequencies. Lift below 1 indicates negative dependence. Exactly 1 corresponds to empirical independence for these two binary events when both margins are positive.

A value near 1 in a finite sample is not proof of independence in a population. Lift also does not estimate the effect of a promotion or a recommendation.

The permitted joint count is bounded: max(0, count(X) + count(Y) − N) ≤ count(X ∪ Y) ≤ min(count(X), count(Y)). The slider respects those bounds.

The practical’s Burger → Ketchup example gives 5% rule support, 33.3% confidence, and lift 3.33. Ketchup’s 10% support is the consequent baseline, not the rule support.

Quick check · The cereal confidence is 66.7%. Is that above its 75% baseline?

No. Lift is about 0.89. Cereal occurs less often among basketball players than it does overall. The arrow does not imply basketball causes that difference.

Lift describes the baseline comparison. Next decide which support and confidence levels a rule must pass.

07A strong rule meets two cutoffs

How do minimum support and confidence work together?

An itemset is frequent when it passes minimum support. A strong rule passes both minimum support and minimum confidence. First find frequent itemsets, then filter the rules made from them. Equality at a cutoff counts as passing.

No. Both conditions must pass. Perfect confidence cannot compensate for insufficient support.

Next: Apriori prunes supersets ↓
08Apriori prunes supersets

Why can an infrequent subset stop a whole branch?

Bread + Coke is a subset of Bread + Coke + Egg; the triple is a superset because it adds Egg. Adding requirements can only keep or reduce the matching baskets. Every basket containing the triple also contains the pair, so the triple’s support count cannot be larger.

The downward closure property

count({Bread, Coke, Egg}) = 2
count({Bread, Coke}) = 2
count({Bread}) = 3

DVD appears once. At a minimum count of 2, every itemset containing DVD must fail. We can discard those branches before counting them.

Source basis: Lecture pp.21–24; textbook §6.2.1. Reading receipts stay fixed when you edit the live lab.

↳
Try it

Inspect Bread + Coke + Egg, then Apple + Bread + Coke. Compare each pair subset with the complete set. Find a failing subset that rules out the triple.

Why this works · a little deeper

The Apriori property says every nonempty subset of a frequent itemset is frequent at the same threshold. Its useful contrapositive says an infrequent itemset has no frequent superset.

The converse fails: frequent singletons do not ensure a frequent pair, and frequent pairs do not ensure a frequent triple. Different subsets can occur in different baskets.

Confidence lacks this general support property. A confidence threshold cannot be substituted for minimum support in arbitrary candidate pruning.

Quick check · If Apple and Bread are frequent separately, must Apple + Bread be frequent?

No. In the four source baskets both singletons pass count 2, but their pair appears only in basket 300 and fails.

Turn that counting property into Apriori’s join, prune and count procedure.

Next: Apriori joins, prunes, counts ↓
09Apriori joins, prunes, counts

How does the algorithm build longer patterns?

Apriori works one itemset length at a time. Cₖ means candidates of length k. Lₖ means candidates that passed support. Join the previous frequent sets, reject missing-subset candidates, then scan the baskets to count survivors.

Why this works · a little deeper

Sort item names consistently. Two frequent (k − 1)-sets can join when their first k − 2 items match. The joined set must have k distinct items. Check every immediate (k − 1)-subset against Lₖ₋₁ before scanning.

The supplied lecture’s A,C,D / B,C,E example is the same pattern of item presence as our named baskets, with D corresponding to DVD.

Apriori can require many database scans and create many candidates. Hash buckets can rule out low-count groups, transaction reduction skips rows that cannot hold longer frequent sets, partitioning collects local candidates then verifies them globally, and sampling trades completeness for speed unless followed by suitable verification. Dynamic counting introduces candidates within a scan.

Quick check · All immediate subsets passed. Can we output the candidate as frequent without counting it?

No. Passing the subset test only makes it a viable candidate. Its own count still has to meet minimum support.

Next: FP-trees share prefixes ↓
11FP-trees share prefixes

How can five baskets fit into a compact tree?

Apriori repeatedly builds candidates. An FP-tree instead compresses baskets into shared ordered paths. First remove infrequent items and choose one global frequency order. A prefix is the shared starting part of a path. Insert each filtered basket; reuse its existing prefix and increase those node counts.

A separate FP-tree example from the lecture

Here f, c, a, b, m and p are abstract item labels in a separate five-basket dataset, not the Apple–Egg abbreviations.

The header lists each item’s count across all five baskets. In the tree, ∅ is the empty root where paths start, not an item. Each node label is item:prefix count. The f → c node’s c:3 counts three baskets with that prefix; c:1 on another branch accounts for c’s fourth occurrence.

Both f and c occur four times. The lecture chooses f before c to break that tie. Keep the same order in every basket.

Why this works · a little deeper

A node count is the number of inserted transactions that share the complete path from the root to that node. It is not necessarily the item’s global support count. The two c nodes carry counts 3 and 1, which add to header c:4.

The first original-data scan obtains support and order. The second constructs the tree. Item header links connect nodes for the same item across branches. The gold inspection highlights the latest inserted path.

Compression depends on common prefixes. FP-growth avoids explicit candidate generation and is often efficient on large data, but branching and conditional trees can still demand substantial memory. “Faster” is not a universal runtime guarantee.

Quick check · Does the f → c node’s c:3 mean c appears only three times in all five baskets?

No. It counts the f,c prefix. Another c:1 starts at the root, so c’s global count is 4.

Next: FP-growth follows a suffix ↓
12FP-growth follows a suffix

Where does a conditional pattern base come from?

The tree is built; now we extract patterns from it. Pick a suffix, the last item of a pattern in the chosen global order. Follow each matching tree node toward the root. Remove the suffix and keep each prefix with that suffix node’s count as its weight: this is the conditional pattern base.

Next: Frequent, closed, maximal ↓
14Frequent, closed, maximal

Which patterns can we keep without losing support information?

Many frequent patterns repeat the same information. A closed frequent itemset has no larger itemset with the same count. A maximal frequent itemset has no frequent larger itemset at all. Keep closed patterns with their counts to recover exact supports of all frequent subsets; maximal patterns generally preserve only which subsets are frequent.

Why this works · a little deeper

A larger itemset that contains X is a proper superset of X. Every maximal frequent itemset is closed, but a closed itemset may still have a frequent extension.

Closed frequent sets and their counts retain the supports of all frequent subsets: the support of X is the maximum count of a closed frequent superset containing X. Maximal sets tell us which subsets are frequent but generally do not preserve their exact counts.

A frequent set of 100 items has 2¹⁰⁰ − 1 nonempty subsets. Direct closed-pattern miners can merge items always appearing together, prune sub-itemsets with matching support, skip redundant projections, and check closure, rather than enumerate everything and filter afterward.

Next: Null transactions change some scores ↓
15Null transactions change some scores

What happens if many baskets contain neither item?

We have counted and summarized patterns. Now check what happens when the background changes. A null transaction contains neither X nor Y. Adding such rows increases the all-basket denominator N, changing support and lift. The X and Y groups stay the same, so both directional confidences stay fixed.

A small teaching example inspired by Chapter 6

Both = 10, X only = 10, Y only = 10
With 0 null rows: N=30, lift=0.75
With 10 null rows: N=40, lift=1.00
With 100 null rows: N=130, lift=3.25
In all three: conf(X→Y)=conf(Y→X)=0.50

These are constructed counts, not measured customers. The point is to hold the co-occurrence evidence fixed while changing the background.

Why this works · a little deeper

A measure is null-invariant if extra neither rows leave it unchanged while all other counts stay fixed. Confidence, all-confidence, max-confidence, Kulczynski, cosine and the imbalance ratio have this property.

Let c₁ = P(Y|X), c₂ = P(X|Y). All-confidence is min(c₁,c₂), max-confidence is max(c₁,c₂), Kulczynski (Kulc) is (c₁+c₂)/2, and cosine is √(c₁c₂). Each lies between 0 and 1 when defined.

IR = |count(X) − count(Y)|
/ [count(X)+count(Y)−count(X∪Y)]

The IR denominator counts rows containing at least one of X or Y. IR = 0 means balanced marginal counts. A large IR warns that the two directions can be very different. Kulc and IR together describe overlap and imbalance.

Null-invariant does not mean every change to the population should be ignored. Lift answers a baseline-relative question; the other scores answer different questions. Kulc = 0.5 is not a general proof of statistical independence.

Quick check · Does adding a null transaction count as a failure among X baskets?

No. It contains no X and never enters the X-conditioned denominator. It does enter the full N denominator for support and baseline frequency.

Confidence stayed fixed while the baseline moved. Next compare observed and expected counts in all four cells.

Next: Observed versus expected ↓
16Observed versus expected

What does the χ² calculation add?

Lift compared observed and expected joint occurrence. Pearson’s chi-square (χ²) statistic compares all four cells: both, X only, Y only and neither. Each contributes (observed − expected)² / expected. Add the four nonnegative contributions to obtain χ².

Why this works · a little deeper

Each expected cell is its row total times its column total divided by N. The figure shows observed and expected counts on one shared count scale. The table shows the four separate contributions.

χ² alone has no positive or negative direction. Compare observed joint count with expected joint count to describe the direction. A large statistic is also sensitive to sample size. Like lift, χ² can change when extra neither rows are added.

A significance claim needs a test setup, degrees of freedom, assumptions, a chosen error rate, and, where relevant, corrections for many mined hypotheses. For a nondegenerate 2×2 table the usual degrees of freedom is 1. χ² > 1 is not a general significance rule. This notebook reports the uncorrected Pearson statistic and no p-value.

Quick check · Can a squared χ² contribution be negative when observed is below expected?

No. The signed discrepancy is negative, but its square is nonnegative. The direction of association must be read from observed versus expected joint counts.

Next: Constraints direct the search ↓
17Constraints direct the search

When can a budget rule prune longer itemsets?

Scores judge discovered rules. Constraints tell the search which itemsets are allowed. An anti-monotone constraint lets us stop after a failure: every larger set fails too. A monotone constraint preserves success as we add items. For sum constraints, the item values matter.

Why this works · a little deeper

Nonnegative values make sum(S) ≤ v anti-monotone and sum(S) ≥ v monotone. A negative item could repair an upper-budget failure, so the pruning would become invalid.

range(S) = max(S) − min(S) cannot shrink as a nonempty set grows. An upper range bound is anti-monotone. A lower range bound is monotone.

A succinct constraint can be enforced by generating only allowed items or itemsets before support counting. For example, max(price) ≤ 120 lets us exclude individually expensive items first.

Convertible constraints can gain a useful pruning property along a chosen item order. Average-value constraints are a key example, but the exact order and direction must be justified. Inconvertible constraints lack such a usable conversion in the specified domain. Sums with unrestricted negative and positive values can lose both simple monotonicity properties.

Apply a valid constraint early when it saves work. Filtering completed output alone produces fewer displayed patterns but does not undo the mining cost.

Quick check · Could adding an item with value −30 repair a set that costs 50 under an upper budget of 35?

Yes. The new sum is 20. That is why nonnegative item values are an explicit assumption for upper-sum anti-monotone pruning.

A broad category occurs at least as often as any of its subtypes. A uniform support cutoff can keep Milk while discarding Skim milk. Lower cutoffs at deeper levels can reveal specific patterns, but they change which comparisons are fair.

Why this works · a little deeper

Multilevel association mining uses a taxonomy such as Dairy → Milk → Skim milk. More specific items often need lower support thresholds.

19Preprocessing creates the items

What should one-hot encoding mean in the practical?

A mining algorithm needs clear yes/no items. One-hot encoding makes one Boolean column per item: True means present, False means absent. Define the row’s unit, remove IDs, and turn numeric ranges into named items when needed. The practical’s BlackFriday rows are product records, so their rules concern those records’ categories and purchase bands.

Why this works · a little deeper

In the small sample, the practical explicitly defines ? as absence. An unknown or unobserved value in another dataset must not automatically become False.

pd.qcut creates quantile bands with roughly equal counts, using the empirical data distribution. pd.cut uses chosen numeric boundaries. Tied values can make quantile boundaries non-unique and need an explicit handling policy.

Use tokens such as Cat=1 and Purchase=High so numeric categories cannot collide with numeric bin labels. Collapsing category-slot labels into Cat=1 means “category 1 appears in any slot”. If slot position matters, retain prefixes such as Category_1=1 instead.

The practical’s zero sentinel denotes no extra category, not product category zero. Keeping an explicit absence token is a deliberate modelling choice. It can dominate output with predictable rules. One-hot encoding also makes mutually exclusive purchase bands unable to co-occur in one record.

Neither Sample Data.csv nor BlackFriday.csv was supplied in this week’s folder. The companion script includes the four documented sample rows and accepts the real BlackFriday file later, without fabricating its results.

The companion’s NoExtraCategory token means both extra category slots are absent. This is a more specific meaning than the practical’s generic zero sentinel, which can appear when either extra slot is absent.

For these nine sorted prices, qcut places three records in each band. Its cut values interpolate empirical quantiles: 1,100 lies between 900 and 1,200; 1,900 lies between 1,800 and 2,100. The bands describe similar row counts, not equal numeric widths.

No. A row contains one product record and its attributes. Basket-level product recommendations require a justified transaction or grouping unit with multiple products per basket.

Next: A small Python workflow ↓
20A small Python workflow

How do the calculations become runnable code?

Return to the four original shopping baskets. The Boolean table is the input; frequent itemsets are the first output; rules are the second. Apriori and FP-growth should agree on the same data and support threshold. They search differently, then apply the same confidence calculation.

Complete four-basket Python example

Install the optional libraries in your Python environment with python3 -m pip install pandas mlxtend. Copy this complete example, or use the companion below.

import pandas as pd
from mlxtend.frequent_patterns import (
    apriori, association_rules)

# 1. Presence rows, without transaction IDs
encoded = pd.DataFrame([
    [1, 0, 1, 1, 0],  # basket 100
    [0, 1, 1, 0, 1],  # basket 200
    [1, 1, 1, 0, 1],  # basket 300
    [0, 1, 0, 0, 1],  # basket 400
], columns=["Apple", "Bread", "Coke",
            "DVD", "Egg"], dtype=bool)

# 2. Itemsets with at least 50% support
freq = apriori(encoded,
    min_support=0.5, use_colnames=True)

# 3. Rules with at least 90% confidence
rules = association_rules(freq,
    metric="confidence", min_threshold=0.9,
    num_itemsets=len(encoded))
rules = rules.sort_values("lift", ascending=False)
print(len(freq), len(rules))  # 9 5

encoded is the yes/no input table; freq holds itemsets and supports; rules holds arrows and their scores. The example prints 9 5. The lab follows your current basket edits, while this printed example uses the fixed source rows.

Why this works · a little deeper

To read the code: import loads tools; a name before = stores a result. The inner lists are rows, columns names the items, and dtype=bool turns 1/0 into True/False. A call such as apriori(...) runs a tool with the supplied input and settings. The comments mark the same three steps shown in the visual.

use_colnames=True preserves readable item names. max_len limits the length of mined itemsets, not the number of rules. The confidence cutoff belongs to association_rules, rather than to apriori.

The code includes num_itemsets, as shown in current mlxtend examples. The companion inspects the function signature so it also works with earlier installations that lack that argument.

For the small practical CSV, validate values before astype(bool): the string "0" is truthy in Python, so casting raw strings directly is unsafe.

Why this works · a little deeper

Actionable rules point toward a useful decision. Trivial rules restate known facts, such as a category hierarchy. Inexplicable rules may deserve investigation but do not immediately imply a worthwhile action.

Rare patterns can have large lift with very few joint observations. Keep absolute counts visible. Evaluate whether patterns persist in fresh periods, whether many comparisons were searched, and whether the item definitions create the relationship mechanically.
