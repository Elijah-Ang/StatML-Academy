01The problem

How rare are the actual positives?

If one in a hundred cases is positive, predicting negative for everybody gets 99% accuracy. It also misses every positive. Start with which cases matter and what each type of mistake costs.

Why this works · a little deeper

The positive class is the case we choose to flag. Sensitivity is recall, TP/(TP+FN). Specificity is TN/(TN+FP). Accuracy can be dominated by negatives when positives are rare. Define the action and error costs instead of treating 99% as automatically useful.

No.

02Intuition and objective

Why can accuracy look high while alerts are unreliable?

Prevalence is the fraction of cases that are actually positive. Precision compares true positives with false alarms among alerts. With the same sensitivity and specificity, rarer positives usually mean lower precision.

Why this works · a little deeper

Precision = TP/(TP+FP). Even a small false-positive rate can generate many false alerts when there are many negatives. Prevalence can change predictive values while sensitivity and specificity stay fixed in a hypothetical comparison. In real new populations, those rates may change too, so verify them rather than assuming only prevalence differs.

Quick check · Can precision change without changing sensitivity or specificity?

Yes, when prevalence changes.

03Worked example

Worked example

Where do 10,000 decisions go?

Out of 10,000 cases at 1% prevalence, 100 are positive and 9,900 negative. Finding 90% of positives gives 90 true alerts. Falsely alerting 5% of negatives gives 495 false alerts.

Quick check · Are 90% of the alerts true positives?

No. Sensitivity is 90%, but precision is about 15.4%.

05Interactive laboratory

What changes when the decision threshold moves?

Lowering the cutoff adds alerts from the same fixed scores. Recall cannot decrease, but false alerts and workload can grow. Precision may change; it is not guaranteed to move in one direction at every step.

06Assumptions and failure modes

What happens when prevalence changes?

Resample training data only. Making an evaluation set artificially balanced changes the positive frequency. Its precision may then look very different from the precision in real use.

Why this works · a little deeper

Assumptions

Evaluation needs a relevant positive frequency and case mix. The positive labels, costs and capacity assumptions must be credible. Preserve appropriate group or time structure.

When the method fails

Keep evaluation prevalence and case mix relevant to deployment, or use justified weighting and explain it. Oversampling before folds can put closely related or duplicated examples into both training and validation. Resampling must happen within training. Do not report balanced-test precision as if it came from the natural deployment population.

09Knowledge checks

Can you reconstruct the three fractions?

For a new positive frequency, recalculate counts first. Then calculate percentages from the right groups. Precision divides by all alerts, not by all actual positives.

A small example · separate from the live dataset
Follow the ideaRead it in plain language
1,000 people, 10% prevalence100 positives
Sensitivity 80%, specificity 90%TP 80, FP 90
Precision80 / 170 ≈ 47.1%
A rare positive class makes accuracy…

Answer and explanation

Potentially misleading. Predicting the majority class can dominate accuracy.

Oversampling belongs…

Answer and explanation

Inside each training fold. Held-out rows must remain untouched and at natural prevalence.

Which curve often reveals rare-positive performance?

Answer and explanation

Precision–recall. The curve compares how many positives we find with how reliable our alerts are.

Threshold choice should use…

Answer and explanation

Validation data and explicit costs. The cutoff is a decision setting chosen during development.

After case-control sampling, probabilities may need…

Answer and explanation

Recalibration to deployment prevalence. Sampling changes the class prior seen during training.
