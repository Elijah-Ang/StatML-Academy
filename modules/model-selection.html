01The Overfitting Trap

Why can extra flexibility hurt new predictions?

More inputs or a more flexible shape can help find a pattern. They can also follow chance details. Choose a model by useful predictions on other data, together with complexity, stability and the task's needs.

02The n-p Puzzle

What if several coefficient choices give the same fit?

If one input column exactly repeats a combination of others, ordinary coefficients may have no unique answer. Nearly repeated information can make the coefficients unstable. A penalty can help stabilize predictions, but changes the fitting rule.

Why this works · a little deeper

A design matrix is the table of model input columns. If its columns are not independent, several coefficient vectors may fit equally well. This is identifiability, a different issue from interpolation or overfitting. Ridge, Lasso or other constraints can support prediction, but they do not prove which overlapping variable has a causal effect.

Quick check · Does a unique Ridge solution prove individual causal effects?

No.

03Picking the Best Team

Why not search every subset?

Trying every group of p predictors means checking 2ᵖ subsets. That grows quickly. For three predictors, there are eight subsets, including none. A large search also gives more chances to find a lucky validation result.

Why this works · a little deeper

Forward selection adds an input at a time. Backward selection removes one at a time. Stepwise or hybrid procedures do both under stated rules. These shortcuts do not guarantee the best of all subsets. Feature selection belongs inside validation folds, so the held-out rows do not influence the chosen subset.

Quick check · Does searching more candidates guarantee a more reliable result?

No. It can increase selection bias.

04The Scorecards

What does each scorecard penalize?

Scorecards judge different things. Adjusted R² penalizes complexity in training fit. AIC and BIC combine likelihood with a parameter penalty. Cross-validation measures errors on rows kept out of each fit. They are not interchangeable.

Follow the ideaRead it in plain language
AICLikelihood fit with a complexity penalty
BICA different, sample-size-dependent penalty
CVOut-of-fit prediction loss

Change degree and compare the fit term, AIC penalty and BIC penalty. Add each penalty to the same fit term by hand before checking the totals.

Why this works · a little deeper

Under comparable likelihood models, AIC = −2 log likelihood + 2k and BIC = −2 log likelihood + k log n, with k fitted parameters and n observations. Shared constants or per-row scaling can produce equivalent score presentations. Lower is better within the relevant comparison. Adjusted R² uses a different scale, with higher better. Mallows' Cp has its own variance estimate and conventions. Name the criterion and parameter count; none automatically guarantees new-data performance.

Quick check · Are all these scores interchangeable?

No. Their objectives and assumptions differ.

05The Shrinking Trick

What does shrinkage trade away?

Regularization adds a penalty to the fitting objective. The model may accept larger training errors to keep coefficients smaller or the rule simpler. This can trade some flexibility for stability.

Why this works · a little deeper

The fitting objective combines error with a penalty. λ=0 removes that penalty; larger λ usually increases its influence. Penalizing coefficients depends on feature units, so choose scaling deliberately and fit it only within training folds. A smaller coefficient is not automatically an unimportant or causally irrelevant input.

Quick check · Does lower training error prove the penalty is too strong?

No. Compare validation behavior.

06Ridge Regression (L2)

Why does Ridge usually keep all predictors?

Ridge adds a penalty for squared coefficient sizes. It pulls weights towards zero smoothly, usually keeping them in the model. The intercept is normally left out of the penalty.

Why this works · a little deeper

This lab's Ridge objective is MSE/2 + λΣβ_j²/2, with the intercept excluded. The sum Σ adds over non-intercept coefficients. Ridge generally does not select a sparse list, although special coefficients can still equal zero. Its stabilizing effect does not guarantee that every penalty value improves validation error.

Quick check · Does Ridge usually perform exact feature selection?

No. Coefficients generally shrink without becoming exactly zero.

07Lasso Regression (L1)

Why can Lasso produce zeros?

Lasso adds a penalty for absolute coefficient sizes. It can make some weights exactly zero. But when inputs share information, a small sample change can alter which input remains.

Why this works · a little deeper

Lasso uses an L1 penalty, a sum of absolute coefficient sizes. Its shape has a corner at zero, which can produce exact zeros. This is selection by the fitting rule, not proof that omitted variables have no effect. Check selection across resamples. Elastic Net combines L1 and squared L2 penalties and may behave differently when inputs overlap.

Quick check · Is a zero Lasso coefficient proof of no relationship?

No. It is a result of a penalized predictive fit.

08Finding the Sweet Spot

Why this works · a little deeper

Use folds suited to the structure: whole related groups together, forward-time splits for forecasting, or suitable class-stratified splits where needed. Refit imputation, scaling, encoding, selection and compression inside each training fold. Nested validation uses an inner loop to select and an outer loop to assess the whole selection process. It reduces selection bias under a suitable design; it does not guarantee a perfectly unbiased estimate in every setting.

Quick check · May λ be chosen using the final test score?

No.

09The "Super-Variable" Secret

How do PCR and PLS differ?

PCR compresses inputs using their spread before predicting the outcome. PLS chooses compressed directions using the inputs' relationship with the outcome. Both must learn those directions inside training data.

A small example · separate from the live dataset
Follow the ideaRead it in plain language

Why this works · a little deeper

PCR means principal component regression. It uses directions of input variation, which may not be the directions useful for predicting y. PLS means partial least squares; it uses input–outcome relationships to construct directions. The number of components is another development choice. Neither method is automatically a causal explanation or a guarantee that useful information is preserved.

Quick check · Does the highest-variance direction necessarily predict y best?

No.

11Common Mistakes

How does repeated evaluation bias our choices?

Repeatedly checking the same evaluation scores can adapt choices to that data's noise. A human making the choices still uses that information. Keep fitting, development selection and final evaluation separate.

Follow the ideaRead it in plain language

Why this works · a little deeper

Choosing settings after viewing scores is still learning from those rows, even without fitting coefficients on them. Many comparisons can overfit development evidence. A final untouched test or an appropriate outer evaluation checks the selected procedure. Report what was searched, the design and uncertainty rather than hiding the number of attempts.

Quick check · Can human choices leak test information?

Yes. Decisions based on test feedback use that information.
