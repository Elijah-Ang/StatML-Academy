Logistic Regression

01It Starts with Data

What is the binary outcome?

The target has two possible answers, called classes. We label them 0 and 1. Logistic regression uses the input clues to estimate the chance of class 1. Only use clues available when the prediction is made. A decision threshold is a cutoff: at or above it, predict 1; below it, predict 0.

02Why Lines Break

Why is an ordinary line awkward for probability?

A straight-line formula can give numbers below 0 or above 1. Those cannot be probabilities. Logistic regression first makes a raw score, then changes it into a number between 0 and 1.

Why this works · a little deeper

The raw score adds up weighted inputs and an intercept. The sigmoid turns that score into a valid probability. A finite score gives a value strictly between 0 and 1. That bound alone does not prove the estimated probability is accurate.

03From a Raw Score to a Probability

What does the sigmoid do?

The sigmoid is an S-shaped curve that converts the raw score into a probability. A score of zero becomes 0.5. Large positive scores approach 1; large negative scores approach 0.

Follow the ideaRead it in plain language
s = 0p = 0.5
s ≈ 1.10p ≈ 0.75
s ≈ −1.10p ≈ 0.25

Try it

Move the input coordinates and follow the gold point along the S-curve. Then move the decision threshold. Watch the two-colour field change while the fitted curve and probability stay fixed.

Why this works · a little deeper

s = intercept + weight₁×input₁ + weight₂×input₂ + … . Then p = 1/(1+e^(−s)). e is a fixed mathematical number used by the exponential function. s is this model's raw score, not a standardized z-score. The model calculates it separately for each row. A teaching slider may inspect that conversion, but training learns weights rather than one free score for every row.

Quick check · Does doubling s double the probability?

No. The mapping is nonlinear.

04Weighted Clues

How do weighted clues form s?

Multiply each input by its weight, then add the starting value, called the intercept. The total is the raw score s. A positive contribution raises s; a negative one lowers it.

Why this works · a little deeper

A weight is also called a coefficient. Each input-times-weight product adds to the raw score. Learn any input scaling from training data. Scaling can help training and make weight penalties more comparable. Large input units do not automatically dominate a fitted linear model because its coefficients account for units. 05Learning from Mistakes

What does learning minimize?

Training changes the weights to reduce log loss, a penalty for poor probability predictions. A very confident wrong answer gets a large penalty. Being right about the label is only part of the task.

True class 1, p = 0.9Loss about 0.105

LearningReduce average training loss

Try it

Set the true-class probability to 0.9, then 0.1. Predict which penalty is larger. The point and receipt calculate this one-case loss; they do not retrain the classifier.

Why this works · a little deeper

Binary log loss is −[y log(p)+(1−y)log(1−p)]. If the true class is 1, probabilities .95, .55 and .05 give losses about .05, .60 and 3.00. These are one-row examples; training usually averages over rows. A regularization penalty can discourage large weights. Gradient descent takes small downhill steps; its learning rate is the step size. Other optimizers can fit logistic regression too. Excessively large steps may fail to settle.

Quick check · Can two classifiers have the same accuracy but different log loss?

Yes. Their confidence can differ.

Next: Drawing the Line ↓
06Drawing the Line

Why does a probability need a decision threshold?

A threshold is the cutoff for choosing a label. At or above it, predict class 1. Below it, predict class 0. Changing the cutoff changes decisions, not the probabilities already fitted by the model.

Why this works · a little deeper

For probability .62, a .50 threshold predicts 1 and a .70 threshold predicts 0. Lower thresholds catch at least as many positives and usually add false alarms on fixed data. Raising a threshold does not guarantee that precision rises at every step. Select it using validation data, error costs, class frequency and capacity. Keep the final test out of that choice.

Quick check · Must the threshold be 0.5?

No.

07Select the Decision Rule on Validation Data

Which mistakes does this threshold create?

The confusion matrix sorts predictions into four groups: correct positives, correct negatives, false alarms and missed positives. Moving the threshold changes these counts. The fitted probabilities stay the same.

Why this works · a little deeper

TP means predicted 1 and actual 1. TN means predicted 0 and actual 0. FP is predicted 1 but actual 0. FN is predicted 0 but actual 1. Accuracy uses all rows; precision uses predicted positives; recall uses actual positives. F1 combines precision and recall and can be undefined under some zero-denominator conventions. The final-test snapshot remains separate from ongoing validation changes.

08The Validation ROC Curve

What does the ROC curve summarize?

The ROC curve compares how many positives we catch with how many negatives we falsely flag. It repeats that comparison across thresholds. It describes ranking tradeoffs, not whether the probabilities are well calibrated.

Why this works · a little deeper

ROC plots recall, TP/(TP+FN), against false-positive rate, FP/(FP+TN). AUC is the area under that curve. It summarizes positive-versus-negative ranking; ties count half. AUC .5 is the random-ranking benchmark, but some useful non-random rankings can also have AUC .5. AUC is not accuracy, calibration or a threshold recommendation. Small validation sets give coarse steps and uncertain estimates.

Quick check · Can a high ROC-AUC coexist with poor probability calibration?

Yes.

09What the Numbers Mean

What is an odds ratio?

Odds compare the chance of yes with the chance of no: p/(1−p). An odds ratio says how those odds multiply when an input rises by one unit, keeping the other inputs fixed. Odds are not probability.

Follow the ideaRead it in plain language
p = 0.2Odds = 0.25
Odds doubled0.5
New probability0.5 / 1.5 ≈ 0.333

Your notebook: Open the visual to inspect the current calculation.

↳
Try it

Read each input weight and its e^weight odds multiplier. Change the data pattern or weight penalty and compare the saved multipliers. A one-unit input change holds the other input fixed.

Why this works · a little deeper

At p=.75, odds are .75/.25=3, or 3 to 1. The raw score s is the natural logarithm of the odds. Adding a weight to that score multiplies the odds by e^weight. This gives the odds ratio: OR=e^weight. OR=2 doubles the odds, not the probability and not the odds ratio itself. Interpretation holds other modeled inputs fixed. For standardized inputs, one unit means one unit on that transformed scale. These associations do not prove that changing an input causes the outcome.

Quick check · Does doubling odds from p = 0.2 produce p = 0.4?

No.
