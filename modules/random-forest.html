01The problem

Why combine several trees?

A random forest combines many trees. Each predicts the same new point, then we combine the answers. Averaging can reduce sensitivity to one tree's splits when their errors are not all the same.

Why this works · a little deeper

One tree's greedy cuts can change sharply after a small data change. Varied trees can average out some of that movement. They still share the original training dataset, so hundreds of trees are not hundreds of independent datasets. Averaging is useful under appropriate diversity; it does not guarantee lower error in every case.

02Intuition and objective

What makes trees different?

Trees differ because they see different bootstrap samples and different candidate inputs at each split. A bootstrap samples rows with replacement. Random input selection is a separate way to vary the trees.

Follow the ideaRead it in plain language

Why this works · a little deeper

Replacement means a row remains available after every draw. One tree may see the same original row several times and omit another. Random candidate features change which questions it can consider at each split. These two mechanisms are distinct, and both affect tree diversity.

04Reasoning with the method

Why does correlation between trees matter?

Averaging helps less when trees make the same errors. Some variation between trees is useful. But making each tree too weak can also make the combined model worse.

Follow the ideaRead it in plain language

Why this works · a little deeper

Compare predictions on the same validation points. Near-diagonal points in a two-tree comparison mean the predictions are similar; shared mistakes matter too. Correlation describes how their outputs move together, not proof of independence. More trees mainly reduce finite-forest randomness. Reducing feature candidates too much can weaken individual trees.

05Interactive laboratory

What is an out-of-bag prediction?

For a training row, an out-of-bag prediction uses only trees that did not include that row in their bootstrap sample. In a tiny forest, there may be no eligible tree for a particular row.

↳
Try it

Select a training row and inspect its voters. Crossed-out trees sampled that row, so they must not contribute to its out-of-bag prediction.

Why this works · a little deeper

OOB means out of bag. For a row, exclude every tree whose bootstrap sample included it, even once. Average only the eligible trees. If there are none, the estimate is unavailable rather than zero. OOB is development evidence; heavy tuning against it can still require outer evaluation.

06Assumptions and failure modes

What problems do more trees not solve?

Adding trees does not fix leaked information, a biased sample or changed future conditions. Repeatedly tuning against the same out-of-bag results can overfit those results too.

Follow the ideaRead it in plain language
LeakageShared across every tree

ImportancePredictive reliance, not causality

Try it

Move one training input. Inspect the combined prediction field and gold-ringed validation mistakes; more trees do not guarantee correct predictions.

Why this works · a little deeper

Assumptions

Training and evaluation must represent the intended use. Inputs must be available at prediction time, and related groups or time must be split appropriately.

When the method fails

Forests can rely on accidental information, extrapolate poorly in regression, or predict probabilities needing calibration. Inputs must be available at prediction time and evaluation must respect related groups or time. Feature importance measures predictive behavior under a specified method, not causation. Correlated inputs can substitute for each other.

Quick check · Can averaging remove a post-outcome feature leak?

No.

How should a forest be tuned and validated?

Compare leaf size, candidate-feature count and other settings using development data. Keep the evaluation conditions realistic. Compare the forest with a useful simple baseline.

Tune leaf constraints and feature sampling under suitable validation. Grow enough trees for sufficiently stable results, rather than treating a larger count as a cure. Keep learned preparation within resampling. Check useful errors, calibration, subgroups and resource cost. Lock the selected procedure before final evaluation.

Bagging mainly reduces…

Answer and explanation

Variation in fitted predictions across training samples. Averaging unstable learners reduces variance when errors are not perfectly correlated.

Random feature subsets help by…

Answer and explanation

Making the trees’ predictions less alike. Less-correlated errors average away more effectively.

OOB error can be misused when…

Answer and explanation

Extensive tuning repeatedly optimises it without outer evaluation. Repeated optimisation makes the estimate part of model selection.

Permutation importance means…

Answer and explanation

How predictions rely on the input when it is disrupted, rather than proof of a cause. Association, correlation and feature dependence shape importance.

A forest regression prediction outside the training range is…

Answer and explanation

Usually poor at extrapolating trends. Tree averages are built from observed leaf outcomes.
