Additional reference · worked numbers below are separate examples from the live notebook.

Before we build an ANOVA model, we must check our assumptions. If the data breaks the rules, standard ANOVA might mislead us.

Normality

Technically, ANOVA assumes the residuals (the error or distance of each point from its group mean) are normally distributed. In practice, researchers often check if the data within each group resembles a bell curve.

Homogeneity of Variance

The spread (variance) of scores should be roughly equal across all groups. ANOVA is robust to minor violations, provided sample sizes are equal.

Independence

Data points must not influence each other. If the same student is tested multiple times, you must use a Repeated-Measures ANOVA instead.

Quick check · Are three scores from the same student three independent students?

No. Repeated observations need a design-aware analysis.

05The F-Statistic

Why divide one mean square by another?

A mean square is a sum of squares divided by its degrees of freedom. F compares the between-group mean square with the within-group mean square, putting both on a comparable variance scale.

$$F = \frac{\text{Between-Group Variance}}{\text{Within-Group Variance}}$$

Degrees of freedom count independent pieces of information after constraints are imposed. Three values with a fixed mean of 10 must sum to 30. If the first two are 8 and 15, the third is forced to be 7. For k groups and N observations, between-group df = k − 1 and within-group df = N − k. Dividing sums of squares by their appropriate df produces mean squares on a comparable variance scale.

We divide our sums of squared deviations (Sum of Squares, SS) by our degrees of freedom to get the Mean Squares (MS), which are variance quantities used in F = MS_between / MS_within. Under the null hypothesis and standard ANOVA assumptions, both estimate the same error variance.

From The Displayed Scores

Group means: A = 80, B = 68, C = 56. Grand mean = 68. Each group has n = 12, so N = 36.

Between-Group Signal

SSbetween = 12[(80 - 68)2 + (68 - 68)2 + (56 - 68)2] = 3456

Within-Group Noise

SSwithin = 132 + 132 + 132 = 396

Applying Degrees of Freedom

dfbetween = 2; dfwithin = 33.
MSbetween = 3456 / 2 = 1728.
MSwithin = 396 / 33 = 12.
F = 1728 / 12 = 144.

A small p-value indicates that the observed result would be unusual under the null hypothesis, assuming the model assumptions hold. It is not the probability that the null is true or a measure of practical importance, so report an effect size and confidence intervals too.

Eta-Squared ($\eta^2$)

Eta-squared shows the proportion of the total variation in scores that is associated with differences between teaching-method groups, without establishing causation. We calculate it using the Sum of Squares from the table:

$\eta^2 = \frac{SS_{between}}{SS_{total}} = \frac{3456}{3852} = 0.90$

About 90% of the variation is associated with the group differences; this does not establish causation.

Notice the new column added to the ANOVA table in the corresponding conceptual example. Depending on your software, this might be reported as Eta Squared, Partial Eta Squared, or $R^2$.

A significant ANOVA is an omnibus test—it tells you a difference exists, but not where. Is A different from B? Is B different from C?

The Multiple Comparisons Problem

If you run three separate t-tests, your 5% chance of a false positive (Type I error) compounds. The more tests you run, the higher the Family-Wise Error Rate.

To follow up safely, we use Post-Hoc tests like Tukey's HSD. Under its assumptions, Tukey's controls the family-wise error rate at the chosen alpha level across the full set of pairwise comparisons. Look at the corresponding conceptual example for the breakdown.

If the gap between methods stays consistent regardless of sleep, the lines in our plot will be roughly parallel. That suggests no interaction.

But if the lines are not parallel, it suggests an interaction. Crossing lines are not technically required for an interaction, but this example uses a strong crossing pattern to make the concept visually obvious: the effect of Method completely flips depending on Sleep.

How to Read the Interaction Plot

  1. Step 1: Check for parallel lines. If lines are roughly parallel, there is likely no interaction. If they cross or clearly change relative position, an interaction is present.
  2. Step 2: Look at overall height. If one line tends to sit higher on average, that suggests a main effect of Sleep.
  3. Step 3: Look at how the ranking changes. Notice which method is best under High Sleep, and then notice how that changes under Low Sleep. Here, the best method completely reverses!

Why Averages Can Hide The Truth

Notice that our Method marginal means are quite close (72, 70, 68). Because the crossover pattern partly cancels out when averaged across sleep levels, the main effect of Method looks modest. Additional reference · worked numbers below are separate examples from the live notebook.

ANOVA is a beautiful, mathematically elegant tool for partitioning variance. But it is only a model of reality.

Assumptions are Key

ANOVA is robust, but severe violations of normality or independence will produce a completely meaningless F-statistic. Always check your residuals.

Look at the Data

Never rely solely on the p-value, tables, or bar charts. Plot the raw data (like our dot plot), check the boxplots, and ensure extreme outliers aren't driving the omnibus result.

Choose the ANOVA variant from the design:

use standard one-way ANOVA for similar variances, Welch ANOVA for unequal variances, Kruskal–Wallis or a robust alternative for strong non-normality or ordinal outcomes, and repeated-measures or mixed models for repeated observations. After a significant omnibus test, use planned contrasts or multiplicity-corrected post-hoc comparisons. Report group intervals and a named effect size such as eta-squared or omega-squared.

In one sentence: ANOVA asks whether the observed between-group variation would be unusual under an equal-means null model, assuming the chosen model and design conditions hold.
