Analysis of Variance (ANOVA)

Compare the averages of several groups. See how the gaps between groups compare with the differences inside each group.

PrerequisitesAverages, fractions, and reading a dot plot.

01The Raw Data

Why do scores differ even within one method?

Students in the same group do not all get the same score. ANOVA compares group averages. It asks whether the gaps between those averages are large compared with the spread inside the groups.

Why this works · a little deeper

One dot is one student's score. A group mean is that group's average; the grand mean is the average over all students. Different students can score differently for many reasons, even when they receive the same teaching method. The drawing describes these scores; it does not establish what caused them.

Quick check · Do different sample means prove different population means?

No. Sampling alone can produce unequal sample means.

Why this works · a little deeper

Keep the average gap fixed and change the spread inside the groups. The gap becomes easier or harder to see. We judge it against the spread and the sample sizes, rather than deciding from the gap alone. A sample is the people we measured; the population is the wider group we want to describe.

Quick check · What happens if spread increases while the gap stays fixed?

The evidence for a mean difference generally weakens.

03The Rules of the Game

Which assumptions support this comparison?

The usual test needs separate, independent observations, roughly bell-shaped errors, and similar error spread in the groups. These are assumptions: conditions the test relies on. A neat-looking plot cannot prove the observations are independent.

Why this works · a little deeper

A residual is a student's score minus their group average. The normal-error assumption concerns these errors, not a requirement that all raw scores share one bell curve. Similar variance means similar squared spread. Equal group sizes can help with some mild departures, but they do not repair dependence. Repeated-measures or mixed models are possible approaches when the same person is measured several times.

Quick check · Are three scores from the same student three independent students?

No. Repeated observations need a design-aware analysis.

Next: Variance Decomposition ↓
04Variance Decomposition

Where does total variation come from?

Compare a student's score with the overall average. Part of that gap comes from their group's average. The rest is the student's gap from their own group average. ANOVA separates these two sources of spread.

A small example · separate from the live dataset
Follow the ideaRead it in plain language
Total SSBetween SS + within SS
BetweenHow far group means sit from the grand mean
WithinHow far students sit from their group means

Your notebook: Open the visual to inspect the current calculation.

↳
Try it

Select a score. Follow the purple distance to its group mean, then the gold distance to the grand mean. Compare those signed distances with the squared totals for all observations.

Why this works · a little deeper

For each student: score − grand mean = (group mean − grand mean) + (score − group mean). Square means multiply a gap by itself. Add the squared gaps across students to get sums of squares, or SS. With the ordinary group-mean fit, total SS = between SS + within SS. Do not interpret the between part as a proven causal effect.

Quick check · Which term grows if group means move apart?

Between-group variation grows, holding within-group variation fixed.

Next: The F-Statistic ↓
05The F-Statistic

Why divide one mean square by another?

Divide each sum of squares by its degrees of freedom, the independent pieces left after estimating averages. That gives a mean square. F divides the between-group mean square by the within-group mean square, comparing them on a similar scale.

A small example · separate from the live dataset
Follow the ideaRead it in plain language
MS betweenSS between / (k − 1)
MS withinSS within / (N − k)
FMS between / MS within

Why this works · a little deeper

F = MS between / MS within. Under the equal-means model and the ordinary test assumptions, both mean squares estimate the same error variance. A large ratio can challenge that model. The denominator is the within-group mean square, not the total sum of squares.

Quick check · If both mean squares are 12, what is F?

F = 1. The two variance estimates are equal.

Next: Degrees of Freedom ↓
06Degrees of Freedom

Why are only some values free?

Degrees of freedom count how many values can still change freely after we have used information to find averages. Once an average is fixed, the gaps from it must add to zero. Some values are therefore no longer free.

Follow the ideaRead it in plain language

Between df3 − 1 = 2
Within df30 − 3 = 27

Why this works · a little deeper

Three values with average 10 must add to 30. If two are 8 and 15, the third must be 7. For k groups and N students, between df = k − 1; within df = N − k; total df = N − 1. The numbers used to estimate averages explain these subtractions.

Quick check · What are the total degrees of freedom here?

N − 1 = 29, which equals 2 + 27.

Next: How Big Is The Effect? ↓
09How Big Is The Effect?

Does a small p-value mean a large effect?

A small p-value means a result at least this extreme would be unusual if all population group means were equal and the test assumptions held. It does not say the gap is big or useful. Check the size of the gap too.

Effect size describes how much separation there is. Eta-squared, η², is SS between / SS total. Quick check · Does p = 0.001 imply a large effect?

No. Report effect size, uncertainty, and practical context as well.

Next: Post-Hoc Testing ↓
10Post-Hoc Testing

Which groups differ?

The overall test asks whether all group averages are equal. It does not tell us which pairs differ. Compare the pairs or planned group combinations separately, allowing for the extra chances of false alarms.

Why this works · a little deeper

Testing many pairs creates more chances to flag a difference by chance. A planned contrast combines groups for a question chosen in advance. A follow-up procedure such as Tukey's HSD can control false alarms across all pairs under its assumptions. Say which comparisons and adjustment you used. Some pairs may remain uncertain even when the overall F test rejects equal means.

Quick check · Does rejecting the omnibus null mean every pair differs?

No. Some pairs may remain still consistent with equal means under the test.

11Enter Two-Way ANOVA

Why add a second factor?

A factor is a category we want to compare, such as teaching method. Adding a second factor, such as study setting, lets us check each combination. We can also ask whether a method works differently in different settings.

It can make effects inseparable or prevent estimation of an interaction.

12Cells & Marginal Means

How do we average across the other setting?

A marginal mean is an average across the other factor. If each combination has the same number of students, average its cell means equally. With unequal group sizes, decide whether you want equal settings or equal students to count.

13The Interaction Effect

What does an interaction mean?

An interaction means one factor's effect changes with the other factor. Compare the gaps between groups in each setting. If the gaps change, there is an interaction in those averages. The lines do not have to cross.

Outdoors: B − A2

Why this works · a little deeper

Indoors, B − A is 8. Outdoors, it is 2. The difference of differences is 2 − 8 = −6. The sign tells us how the gap changes. Nonparallel sample lines show an interaction in those sample averages; a population claim still needs uncertainty and a suitable model. Small random departures from parallel lines are not proof on their own.

Quick check · Can two rising, nonparallel lines show interaction?

Yes. The change in the gap is what matters.

Next: Caveats & Recap ↓
16Caveats & Recap

When should I choose a different analysis?

Choose the method for the data you actually have. Welch ANOVA allows unequal group spreads. Repeated people or groups of related people need methods that handle that link. Using ranks changes the question; it does not fix related observations.

Why this works · a little deeper

Inspect raw points, group spreads and unusual scores. Check why an unusual value occurred rather than deleting it for a preferred result. Rank methods such as Kruskal–Wallis concern distributional or rank differences, not automatically the same mean comparison as ANOVA. Report group estimates, intervals, a named effect size, and any adjusted follow-up comparisons. Match repeated or clustered data with an appropriate design-aware method.

Quick check · Does switching to ranks fix dependent observations?

No. Dependence still needs to be handled.
