[anova]
A group mean summarizes a teaching method, but individual students still differ. ANOVA asks whether the gaps between group means are large relative to this ordinary within-group variation. | Observation~One student's score;Group mean~Average score for one method;Grand mean~Average of all students | Do different sample means prove different population means? | No. Sampling alone can produce unequal sample means.
Judge a gap against its background spread. A five-point gap is easier to distinguish when students within each group are similar than when their scores vary widely. | Same mean gap~5 points;Within-group SD of 2~Relatively distinct groups;Within-group SD of 20~Substantial overlap | What happens if spread increases while the gap stays fixed? | The evidence for a mean difference generally weakens.
The usual F test assumes independent errors, a suitable normal error model within groups, and a common error variance. Independence comes from the study design; a tidy plot cannot establish it. | Independent errors~Check assignment and sampling;Similar variances~Inspect group spreads;Unusual observations~Investigate their origin | Are three scores from the same student three independent students? | No. Repeated observations need a design-aware analysis.
Each deviation from the grand mean has two parts: the group mean's deviation and the student's deviation from that group mean. Their squared totals give between-group and within-group variation. | Total SS~Between SS + within SS;Between~How far group means sit from the grand mean;Within~How far students sit from their group means | Which term grows if group means move apart? | Between-group variation grows, holding within-group variation fixed.
A mean square is a sum of squares divided by its degrees of freedom. F compares the between-group mean square with the within-group mean square, putting both on a comparable variance scale. | MS between~SS between / (k − 1);MS within~SS within / (N − k);F~MS between / MS within | If both mean squares are 12, what is F? | F = 1. The two variance estimates are equal.
Once a mean is fixed, not every deviation can vary freely: the deviations must sum to zero. Estimating k group means uses k degrees of freedom. | Three groups, N = 30~k = 3;Between df~3 − 1 = 2;Within df~30 − 3 = 27 | What are the total degrees of freedom here? | N − 1 = 29, which equals 2 + 27.
Build F one division at a time. Keep the numerator and denominator degrees of freedom attached to their mean squares; swapping them changes the answer. | Between SS = 120, df = 2~MS = 60;Within SS = 270, df = 27~MS = 10;F~60 / 10 = 6 | With MS between 24 and MS within 8, what is F? | F = 3.
An ANOVA table is a calculation receipt. Rows identify sources of variation; columns show the sum of squares, degrees of freedom, mean square, and test result. | Source~Between / within / total;Scale~SS → divide by df → MS;Test~F uses the first two mean squares | Does the total row have its own F test? | No. The omnibus F compares between and within mean squares.
A p-value describes incompatibility with equal population means under the test assumptions. Effect size describes how large the separation is. A large sample can give a small p-value for a modest effect. | p-value~Evidence under a null model;η²~SS between / SS total;Practical importance~Compare with a meaningful score difference | Does p = 0.001 imply a large effect? | No. Report effect size, uncertainty, and practical context as well.
The omnibus test says that not all means are equal; it does not identify the pairs. Planned contrasts or multiplicity-adjusted comparisons answer more specific questions. | Omnibus question~Are all population means equal?;Pairwise question~How much does A differ from B?;Multiplicity~Adjust the family of comparisons | Does rejecting the omnibus null mean every pair differs? | No. Some pairs may remain compatible with equal means.
A second factor lets us study another explanation and whether the first factor behaves differently across it. A balanced two-way design observes every combination with equal replication. | Factor A~Teaching method;Factor B~Study setting;Cell~One method in one setting | What is a missing combination a problem for? | It can make effects inseparable or prevent estimation of an interaction.
A marginal mean averages across levels of the other factor. In a balanced design, the simple average of cell means works; with unequal sizes, the weighting and target population matter. | Method A indoors~60;Method A outdoors~70;Equal-weight marginal mean~65 | If one setting has many more students, is every average still 65? | No. A student-weighted average differs from an equal-setting average.
An interaction means the effect of one factor changes with the other factor. Compare differences, not just heights. Crossing lines are a strong illustration, but lines do not have to cross. | Indoors: B − A~8;Outdoors: B − A~2;Difference of differences~2 − 8 = −6 | Can two rising, nonparallel lines show interaction? | Yes. The change in the gap is what matters.
For a balanced two-way model, total variation separates into factor A, factor B, their interaction, and residual error. In unbalanced designs, sums of squares depend on the model and testing convention. | Explained parts~A + B + A×B;Unexplained part~Residual SS;Total~Sum of all four in the balanced design | Is the interaction just another name for residual error? | No. It is a modeled systematic difference in effects.
Read the design and interaction before interpreting isolated main effects. Then report estimates and intervals alongside the F tests, using comparisons that match the scientific question. | First~What was randomized or sampled?;Then~Does the effect depend on context?;Finally~How large and uncertain is each contrast? | Why can one overall method effect be misleading? | An average can conceal effects that differ substantially by setting.
Choose a method that matches the design and target. Welch ANOVA allows unequal variances; repeated or clustered observations need dependence-aware models; rank methods answer different distributional questions. | Unequal variances~Consider Welch ANOVA;Repeated people~Repeated-measures or mixed model;Ordinal outcome~Consider an appropriate rank or ordinal model | Does switching to ranks fix dependent observations? | No. Dependence still needs to be handled.

[correlation]
Paired means both measurements belong to the same observational unit. Each dot combines that unit's x and y; shuffling one column destroys the original relationship. | Student A~Hours 2, score 55;Student B~Hours 5, score 73;Pairing rule~Keep each student's values together | May I sort each column separately before computing r? | No. That creates pairs that were never observed.
Start with the scatterplot. Direction tells you whether values tend to rise together; form tells you whether the relationship is straight, curved, or something else. | Positive direction~Higher x tends to accompany higher y;Negative direction~Higher x tends to accompany lower y;Curved form~A straight-line summary may miss it | Can a strong U-shaped relationship have r near zero? | Yes. Pearson r summarizes linear association.
Pearson r standardizes covariance by the two standard deviations. It lies between −1 and 1, is unitless, and describes linear association rather than prediction accuracy or cause. | r = +1~All points on an increasing line;r = 0~No linear association in the sample;r = −1~All points on a decreasing line | Does converting metres to centimetres change r? | No, provided the conversion is a positive linear rescaling.
The usual test asks whether the population linear correlation ρ is zero. Its p-value uses an independent-pairs model and distributional assumptions; it is not the probability that ρ equals zero. | Null~ρ = 0;Sample statistic~r;Common test~t = r√((n − 2)/(1 − r²)) | Does failing to reject prove independence? | No. Nonlinear dependence may remain, and the study may be imprecise.
Subtract each mean, then multiply the two deviations. Pairs in matching directions contribute positively; opposite directions contribute negatively. Normalization makes the total comparable across units. | Above both means~Positive product;Below both means~Positive product;Above x, below y~Negative product | What is the product for deviations +2 and −3? | −6. This pair pulls the covariance downward.
One number cannot preserve the shape of a cloud. A curved relationship can be systematic even when upward and downward linear contributions cancel. | Linear trend~Pearson r can summarize it;Monotonic curve~Ranks may help;U-shape~Plot it and model the curve | Would Spearman correlation fully describe a symmetric U-shape? | No. Spearman also targets monotonic association.
An unusual point can strongly affect the means, covariance, and correlation. Inspect it and compare with and without it, but keep or remove it for a defensible reason, not for a preferred result. | Outlier~Unusual observed value;Influence~Large change in the result;Sensitivity~Report both justified analyses | Should a point be deleted because it lowers r? | No. Check measurement, sampling, and the scientific context.
Report r with sample size, uncertainty, and the plot. Statistical evidence and useful predictive strength are different questions. | Estimate~r and its direction;Uncertainty~An appropriate interval or test;Context~Sampling, shape, and unusual points | Can a weak correlation be statistically detectable in a large sample? | Yes. Detectability does not establish practical importance.
Two quantities can move together because one affects the other, both share a cause, or selection creates an association. Correlation alone cannot distinguish these explanations. | Shared cause~Hot weather raises ice-cream sales and swimming;Observed association~Sales and drownings can rise together;Causal conclusion~Needs a credible design and assumptions | Does controlling one variable automatically remove all confounding? | No. Important unmeasured or misspecified causes may remain.
Choose a summary that matches the relationship and measurement scale. Pearson targets linear association; Spearman and Kendall target ordered association. Always inspect the data and dependence structure first. | Pearson~Linear association of numerical values;Spearman~Association of ranks;Kendall~Agreement of pairwise ordering | Which is natural for a monotonic relation measured on an ordinal scale? | A rank-based measure may fit the question better.

[chi-square]
A contingency table counts how often category combinations occur. The independence test compares those observed counts with the counts expected if the two categorical variables were independent. | Row variable~Treatment A or B;Column variable~Recovered or not;Cell~Number of people in one combination | Should percentages replace counts in the chi-square calculation? | No. Use counts; percentages can help interpretation.
Independence means the outcome proportions are the same across row categories in the population. Expected counts preserve the observed row and column totals while applying that no-association model. | Overall recovery~60 of 100 people;A group size~40 people;Expected A recoveries~40 × 0.60 = 24 | Does independence require equal row sizes? | No. Expected counts account for unequal margins.
The row total tells us how many opportunities the cell has. The column total divided by the grand total gives the overall category proportion. Multiplying them gives its expected count. | Row total~40;Column proportion~60 / 100;Expected count~40 × 60 / 100 = 24 | With row total 50 and column share 30%, what is expected? | 15 observations.
Each cell contributes (observed − expected)² / expected. Squaring prevents cancellation; dividing by expected makes a difference relative to the count scale. The statistic adds the cell contributions. | Observed~30;Expected~24;Contribution~(30 − 24)² / 24 = 1.5 | Can a cell contribution be negative? | No. Every squared contribution is nonnegative.
The p-value is the upper-tail area beyond the observed statistic in a chi-square reference distribution. A two-by-two table has one degree of freedom because its margins constrain the remaining cells. | Degrees of freedom~(rows − 1)(columns − 1);For a 2×2 table~1;Tail area~Probability of a statistic at least this large under H₀ | Is the p-value the chance the variables are independent? | No. Independence is assumed when computing the tail probability.
A small p-value challenges independence but gives neither a causal explanation nor the size of association. Examine cell proportions, residuals, and an effect-size measure such as Cramér's V. | Test~Evidence against independence;Cramér's V~Strength of association;Cell pattern~Where observed and expected differ | Does chi-square tell us which variable causes the other? | No. Causality depends on design and additional assumptions.
The approximation can be poor with sparse expected counts. Paired responses, survey weights, and repeated people also need methods that respect the design. | Sparse 2×2 counts~Consider an exact test;Paired binary responses~Consider a paired method;Weighted or clustered survey~Use design-aware inference | Are 100 visits by ten people 100 independent people? | No. The repeated observations violate the ordinary independence assumption.

[probability-sampling]
A population parameter is fixed for the population you defined. A statistic changes with the sample. Repeating the sampling process shows how much the estimate varies. | Population~All eligible units;Parameter~Their true mean;Statistic~The mean of one sample | Which changes when you draw another sample: parameter or statistic? | The statistic changes; the defined population parameter stays fixed.
Discrete probabilities add over outcomes. A continuous density gives probability through area over an interval, not through the curve's height at one point. Frequency is what actually occurred in a sample. | Discrete mass~P(X = 2);Continuous probability~P(1 < X < 2);Observed frequency~Count / sample size | Can a density curve be taller than one? | Yes. Its total area must be one; its height need not be.
Standard deviation describes individuals. Standard error describes an estimator across repeated samples. For an independent sample mean, estimated SE is the sample SD divided by √n. | Sample SD~12 points;Sample size~36 people;Estimated SE~12 / 6 = 2 points | What happens to SE when n quadruples, with SD fixed? | It halves.
Take one sample, calculate its mean, and put one dot on a second plot. Repeating this produces a sampling distribution of means, rather than a distribution of individual observations. | One population~Source of observations;One sample~n observations;One dot~That sample's mean | Does a histogram of 200 sample means represent 200 people? | No. Each mean summarizes a separate sample.
Larger independent samples usually give more stable means. The square-root rule has diminishing returns: doubling precision requires about four times as many observations under the same design. | n = 25, SD = 10~SE = 2;n = 100, SD = 10~SE = 1;Held constant~Population and sampling design | Does increasing the number of simulation repeats reduce the SE of each mean? | No. It estimates the sampling distribution more precisely.
The familiar SE formula assumes independent information. Clustered observations can share errors, and heavy tails can make small-sample approximations unreliable. More rows need not mean proportionally more information. | Independent people~Each contributes separate information;Repeated classmates~Some information overlaps;Consequence~Use the actual sampling design | Can a thousand correlated rows act like a thousand independent rows? | No. Their effective information can be much smaller.
Define whom you want to describe, who can enter the sample, and how selection happens. Random sampling supports population representation; random assignment supports a causal comparison. They solve different problems. | Sampling frame~Who could be selected?;Selection rule~How were units chosen?;Estimator~How do weights and design enter? | Does random treatment assignment make a convenience sample representative? | No. It does not change how participants entered the study.
An estimate needs its population, sampling design, sample size, and uncertainty beside it. A precise estimate from a biased sample can still miss the target population. | Estimate~Mean in outcome units;Uncertainty~SE or interval with method;Scope~Population, coverage, and limitations | Can a tiny SE rule out selection bias? | No. SE describes sampling variability under the assumed design.
Ask what is varying. If you describe how people differ, use SD. If you describe how a mean would vary across repeated samples, use SE. | Individual spread~SD;Mean-estimate spread~SE;Example: SD 15, n 25~SE = 3 | For SD = 8 and n = 16, what is SE? | 8 / √16 = 2, under independent sampling.

[confidence-hypothesis-testing]
An estimate is one value produced by noisy data. An interval shows the range compatible with the procedure and assumptions; its width reflects precision, not the size of the effect alone. | Estimated effect~4 points;Study A SE~1 point;Study B SE~3 points | Which study gives the narrower interval for the same estimate? | Study A, because its standard error is smaller.
A confidence interval describes the long-run coverage of an interval procedure. A p-value describes a tail probability under a stated null model. Neither is a probability that the fixed null hypothesis is true. | 95% confidence~95% coverage over repeated applications;Small p~Observed statistic unusual under H₀;Not supplied~Probability that H₀ is true | Does a 95% interval assign 95% probability to this fixed parameter? | Not in the usual frequentist interpretation.
For a mean with known population SD and independent normal observations, use SE = σ/√n, z = (estimate − null)/SE, and estimate ± 1.96 SE for a 95% interval. Estimated SD usually requires a t reference instead. | Estimate~4;Known σ = 10, n = 100~SE = 1;95% interval~4 ± 1.96 = [2.04, 5.96] | What is z when the null is zero in this example? | z = 4 / 1 = 4.
Alpha controls false rejection under the null. Power is the probability of detecting a particular alternative under a planned design. It changes with effect size, sample size, variability, and the decision rule. | True null rejected~Type I error;Specified alternative missed~Type II error;Power~1 − β | Is power the probability that an observed result is true? | No. Power is a prospective property for a specified alternative.
With the same variability, increasing n shrinks SE and narrows the interval. It can make a small effect statistically detectable without making it practically useful. | n = 25, σ = 10~SE = 2;n = 100, σ = 10~SE = 1;Practical threshold~Must come from the application | Can an enormous study detect a trivial effect? | Yes. Statistical precision and practical importance are separate.
Dependence, searching many outcomes, optional stopping, and a poor model can invalidate the advertised error rate. Changing the method after seeing the result also changes what the nominal p-value means. | Dependence~Rows share information;Multiplicity~Many opportunities for a small p;Optional stopping~Repeated unplanned decisions to stop | Does a multiplicity adjustment repair an invalid independence assumption? | No. Each source of invalidity needs its own remedy.
Choose the question, meaningful effect, sampling or assignment plan, outcomes, and analysis before inspecting results. Prespecification makes the later comparison interpretable. | Question~What effect and population?;Design~What observations are independent?;Analysis~Which tests and correction family? | Should the final hypothesis be whichever outcome looks most significant? | No. That is outcome selection and needs explicit exploratory treatment.
Report the estimate, interval, model, p-value, and practical context. Failure to reject means the evidence was insufficient for that test; it does not establish that an effect is zero. | Effect~4 points;Interval~Shows precision and plausible magnitudes;Conclusion~Compare with a meaningful effect threshold | Can a wide interval including zero prove equivalence? | No. Equivalence needs a planned margin and an appropriate analysis.
Interpret an interval and p-value together without replacing magnitude with a binary label. A result may be inconclusive, precise but small, or large but uncertain. | Estimate~0.2 units;95% interval~[0.1, 0.3];Meaningful effect~2 units | Is this precise small estimate necessarily important? | No. It is far below the stated practical threshold.

[time-series-analysis]
Time gives observations an order and often makes nearby errors related. A future value cannot be used to help predict an earlier value in an honest forecasting evaluation. | Ordinary row label~May be interchangeable;Timestamp~Determines available history;Forecast target~A later value at a stated horizon | Is a random row split always safe for forecasting? | No. It can allow future information into training.
Look for trend, repeating seasons, unusual events, and changing variability before fitting. The plot helps choose a model and identify data problems, but it cannot prove that a pattern will continue. | Trend~Longer-term movement;Seasonality~Pattern repeating at a known period;Break~Change in the data-generating process | Is every repeated-looking wiggle a seasonal pattern? | No. Check repeated cycles and the underlying process.
An additive illustration writes the series as trend + seasonality + remainder. The simulated components here are known because we created them; real-data decomposition estimates components and may be ambiguous. | Trend at one time~20;Seasonal contribution~+3;Remainder −1~Observed value 22 | Must seasonal amplitude stay fixed in every time-series model? | No. A multiplicative or transformed model can represent changing amplitude.
Weak stationarity means a stable mean, finite variance, and covariance depending on lag rather than calendar time. A flat-looking plot alone cannot establish these properties. | Mean~Stable over time;Variance~Stable and finite;Dependence~A function of lag | Does removing a trend automatically establish stationarity? | No. Variance, seasonality, or dependence may still change.
First differencing subtracts the previous observation. Seasonal differencing subtracts the observation one seasonal period earlier. These can remove some structure, but unnecessary differencing can create problems. | Values~20, 23, 22;First differences~3, −1;Monthly seasonal difference~yₜ − yₜ₋₁₂ | How many first differences come from five observations? | Four.
A lag is a time gap. Autocorrelation compares the series with a shifted version of itself, so lag 12 in monthly data compares the same month across years. | Lag 1~Previous month;Lag 12~Previous year's same month;ACF~Correlation-like dependence summary | Does a high lag-12 ACF by itself prove a causal annual mechanism? | No. It is a descriptive pattern requiring context.
Use forecasts made only from information available at each origin. Compare with simple baselines and match the evaluation horizon to the actual decision. | Train~Earlier observations;Validate~Later forecast origins;Baseline~Last value or last season | May a twelve-step forecast use the actual value from step eleven? | Not for a fixed-origin twelve-step evaluation; that would change the task.
Forecast residuals should not retain predictable structure that the model claims to explain. Check bias, changing spread, and autocorrelation, while allowing for sampling noise. | Mean residual~Systematic over- or underprediction;Residual ACF~Remaining temporal structure;Spread~Whether uncertainty changes | Must every sample residual autocorrelation be exactly zero? | No. Small random values occur even under an adequate model.
Report the horizon, origins, baseline, metric, uncertainty method, and known limitations. A prediction interval concerns future observations and relies on assumptions about future conditions. | Horizon~How far ahead?;Evaluation~Which chronological origins?;Uncertainty~How was the interval constructed? | Does a good one-step score guarantee good annual forecasts? | No. Different horizons are different prediction tasks.
A process can change after training: policies, behavior, sensors, or seasons may shift. Monitor performance and input conditions, then investigate before retraining or changing the model. | Break~Persistent level or relationship change;Drift~Distribution changes over time;Response~Investigate, validate, then update | Does input drift alone prove prediction failure? | No. It is a warning to examine outcomes and model performance.

[multiple-linear-regression]
Several available clues can improve a prediction or help describe an adjusted association. Each row still has one outcome; the model now combines several predictors. | Inputs~Study hours and prior score;Target~Exam score;Model~One weighted sum | Does adding a predictor guarantee better new-data predictions? | No. It can add noise or overfitting.
The intercept supplies a starting value, and each predictor contributes its value times a coefficient. Add all contributions to obtain the predicted mean. | Intercept~20;2 × hours, hours = 4~8;0.5 × prior score, prior = 60~30;Prediction~58 | What happens if only hours increase by one? | The prediction rises by 2, holding the other input fixed.
Changing one coefficient changes the model's sensitivity to that predictor. A two-dimensional slice holds the other predictor fixed; it does not display the whole fitted surface. | Coefficient β₁~Change per unit of x₁;Coefficient β₂~Change per unit of x₂;Slice~Fix x₂ and vary x₁ | Does a slice show every observation at its own second input? | No. Observations can sit away from a fixed-input slice.
Least squares chooses coefficients together to minimize the sum of squared vertical residuals. A unique unregularized solution requires independent columns in the design matrix. | Residual~Observed − predicted;Objective~Sum of squared residuals;Identifiability~No exact redundant predictor column | Can two identical columns have uniquely determined separate coefficients? | No. Their combined effect can be identified, but their separate weights cannot.
Holding constant is a comparison inside the model: change one predictor while fixing the others. It is not automatically a feasible intervention or a causal effect. | Compare~x₁ = 3 versus x₁ = 4;Keep fixed~Same x₂ value;Interpretation~Adjusted model association | If two predictors nearly always move together, is this comparison well supported? | It may rely on sparse or nonexistent combinations.
A prediction is a receipt of contributions. Keeping the units visible makes both the calculation and the meaning easier to check. | Starting value~10;Hours contribution~3 × 4 = 12;Experience contribution~2 × 5 = 10;Total~32 | If experience becomes 6, what is the new prediction? | 34, with the other input fixed.
An unadjusted association can change after another variable is included. That variable may explain a shared pattern, but interpretation depends on the causal structure and measurement quality. | Unadjusted~Outcome related to x₁;Adjusted~Compare x₁ at a fixed x₂;Caution~Adjustment can also introduce bias | Should every available variable be adjusted for in causal work? | No. Mediators, colliders, and post-outcome variables need careful treatment.
Training R² cannot fall when an unpenalized least-squares model adds predictors. Adjusted R² adds a complexity penalty, while held-out error asks about new-data prediction directly. | Training R²~In-sample fit;Adjusted R²~Fit with a parameter penalty;Validation error~Prediction on unused development rows | Is the largest training R² a sufficient selection rule? | No. It rewards unnecessary flexibility.
Read coefficients with units and uncertainty, then assess assumptions, overall fit, and validation. Large coefficient uncertainty can reflect collinearity even when predictions remain useful. | Coefficients~Adjusted associations;Intervals~Uncertainty under the model;Validation~Prediction beyond training rows | Does a small coefficient p-value establish causation? | No. The study design and causal assumptions remain necessary.

[polynomial-regression]
A straight line can miss a smooth curve. Polynomial regression lets the predicted mean bend while keeping the coefficients in an additive linear model. | Linear term~x;Quadratic term~x²;Possible pattern~A U-shaped mean | Is quadratic regression linear in its coefficients? | Yes. The unknown coefficients still enter as a weighted sum.
A simple baseline tells us what extra complexity must beat. Compare a straight line and a constant mean before celebrating a flexible curve. | Constant baseline~Training mean;Linear baseline~Intercept + slope × x;Fair comparison~Same held-out rows | Is a complicated curve useful if validation error rises? | Usually not for this predictive task.
Powers are new columns derived from the same input. They expand the shapes the model can represent; they do not add new independent observations. | x = 2~Original feature;x² = 4~Squared feature;x³ = 8~Cubed feature | Does creating five powers multiply the sample size by five? | No. The number of observations is unchanged.
Each coefficient weights one basis function, and their sum gives the curve. Individual polynomial coefficients are hard to interpret alone because their contributions act together. | b₀~Vertical level;b₁x~Linear contribution;b₂x²~Quadratic contribution | Should a quadratic coefficient be interpreted without the other terms? | No. Inspect the combined curve and its domain.
Residuals show what the curve missed. A systematic bend suggests a mean-form problem; a funnel suggests changing variance. A small training residual does not establish generalization. | Residual~y − ŷ;Curve in residuals~Possible missing nonlinear structure;Funnel~Possible nonconstant variance | Does random-looking residual scatter prove causal validity? | No. It checks only some aspects of the fitted model.
A flexible polynomial can chase training noise, especially near the edges. Validation asks whether the extra bends help on rows that did not choose the coefficients. | More flexibility~Lower possible training error;Risk~Unstable fitted shape;Check~Held-out development error | Why is extrapolation especially risky for high-degree polynomials? | Powers can grow rapidly outside the observed range.
Fit candidate degrees on training data and compare them on validation data or cross-validation. Choose the degree before inspecting the final test. | Train~Estimate coefficients;Validation~Choose degree and other settings;Final test~Evaluate the locked procedure | May the final test select the best degree? | No. That would turn it into another validation set.
Lock the degree, preprocessing, coefficients or refitting rule, and evaluation metric. A final result evaluates that complete procedure, not just a curve label. | Locked choice~Degree and preprocessing;Locked fit~The selected model;Next evidence~A fresh final evaluation | If the final result prompts a model revision, what is needed next? | New independent evaluation, or an explicitly exploratory interpretation.

[regression-diagnostics]
R² is a summary of fit, not a certificate that assumptions hold. Different residual patterns can share a similar R² while requiring different scientific responses. | High R²~Much training variation explained;Not guaranteed~Correct mean form or independent errors;First check~Plot residuals | Can high R² coexist with a curved residual pattern? | Yes. The model can fit broadly while missing systematic structure.
A residual is the observed outcome minus its fitted value. Linking the same row between the original and residual plots makes that subtraction visible. | Observed y~12;Fitted ŷ~9;Residual~+3 | What does a negative residual mean? | The model predicted too high.
An outlier has an unusual residual; leverage concerns unusual predictor values; influence concerns how the fit changes. A point can have one property without all three. | Outlier~Far vertically from the fit;Leverage~Unusual input location;Influence~Large change after removal | Should every high-leverage point be deleted? | No. Investigate and report a justified sensitivity analysis.
Choose the diagnostic to match the question. Residual plots inspect mean and spread; Q–Q plots inspect a distributional reference; leverage and VIF address different input issues. | Residual/fitted~Mean form and spread;Q–Q~Error-distribution compatibility;VIF~Predictor redundancy | Must predictors themselves be normal for ordinary least squares? | No. Normal-error assumptions concern some forms of inference, not predictor normality.
A curve suggests the mean function missed structure; a funnel suggests changing error variance. Treat these as clues and validate a justified repair. | Curve~Consider a nonlinear mean;Funnel~Consider variance modeling or robust inference;After repair~Recheck and validate | Does every funnel require a logarithm? | No. The response depends on the outcome scale and scientific model.
Residual plots cannot establish independent sampling or causal identification. Two observations can have related errors even when their scatterplot looks ordinary. | Same patient~Repeated dependence;Nearby times~Temporal dependence;Unmeasured cause~Causal ambiguity | Can a clean residual plot repair a bad sampling design? | No.
Move from symptom to investigation to a justified change. Keep a record of the original model so a sensitivity comparison remains interpretable. | Observe~Residual pattern;Investigate~Measurement, design, model form;Respond~Refit and validate a reasoned alternative | Is removing the worst residual always a valid repair? | No. It may hide a real population feature.
Report the diagnostic finding, why the response was justified, and how estimates or predictions changed. Distinguish uncertainty in a mean from uncertainty in a new individual outcome. | Confidence interval~Mean-response uncertainty;Prediction interval~New-outcome uncertainty;Sensitivity note~What changed and why | Which interval is usually wider at the same input? | The prediction interval, because it includes individual outcome variation.
Match the action to the symptom and the scientific context. Some observations should be retained and investigated rather than automatically transformed or removed. | Curved residuals~Investigate mean form;Repeated units~Model dependence;Data-entry error~Correct against source evidence | A valid rare observation shifts the fit. What should you report? | The observation's context and a sensitivity analysis, rather than silently deleting it.

[bias-variance]
A fitted model depends on both its modeling assumptions and the particular training sample. New outcomes also contain variation that the chosen inputs cannot predict. | Bias~Systematic average prediction error;Variance~Fit changes across samples;Noise~Unpredictable outcome variation | Can perfect training fit remove future outcome noise? | No.
An overly rigid model can miss a stable pattern in the population. Its average prediction can be wrong even if different training samples produce similar fits. | True mean~A curve;Model family~Only straight lines;Consequence~Systematic mismatch | Would collecting more data always remove this model bias? | No. The model family may still be too restrictive.
A flexible model can change substantially when the training observations change. That sensitivity is variance; it is different from the spread of the raw outcomes. | Same true process~Generate another training sample;Refit~Same algorithm and settings;Compare~Predictions at the same input | Does a wiggly line alone prove high variance? | No. Variance concerns behavior across training samples.
For squared error at a fixed input, expected prediction error decomposes into squared bias, prediction variance, and irreducible noise under the stated model. It is not a universal decomposition for every loss. | Expected squared error~Bias² + variance + noise;Expectation~Across training samples and new noise;Location~A fixed prediction input | Does the same additive formula automatically hold for classification accuracy? | No. The loss and assumptions matter.
A textbook U-shape is a useful possibility, not a law. Observed validation curves can be noisy, flat, irregular, or shaped by the data and model family. | Training curve~Usually improves with flexibility;Validation curve~Must be measured;Selection~Include uncertainty and simplicity | Should we draw a U-shaped curve without fitting models? | Only as a clearly labeled conceptual illustration.
