Before building a model, count the target classes. There are 8 Yes rows and 6 No rows, so the majority classifier predicts Yes for every case.

majority accuracy = largest class count ÷ all rows

This baseline is intentionally dull. That is its job. A new classifier that cannot beat it has not earned its electricity.

Class imbalance warning.

If 95% of rows are No, always predicting No scores 95% accuracy while learning nothing about Yes. Accuracy needs company.

If a dataset has 70 Yes rows and 30 No rows, what does the majority classifier predict?

Yes for every row, producing 70% training accuracy.

A rule for every exact temperature would memorise the training sample and fail on a new value such as 23.6°C. One-R therefore discretises numeric values into intervals.

Sort rows by the numeric feature. 2Group neighbouring values into sufficiently reliable buckets. 3Assign each bucket its majority class. 4Merge or adjust unstable neighbours according to the chosen implementation. One possible training-only discretisation Temperature ≤ 22.5 → Yes 22.5 < Temperature ≤ 27.5 → Yes Temperature > 27.5 → No

Leakage alert.

Fit cut-points using training data only. Choosing intervals after inspecting test labels is answer-shopping in a lab coat.

Cross-validation gives multiple views of performance. Use the right metric for your goal, check stability across folds, and decide whether to proceed, tune, or collect more data.

Choose the right metric

Pick the metric that matches the problem and the cost of mistakes.

Overall accuracy

Focus when classes are balanced and both types of errors matter similarly.

Best for: General performance check, balanced datasets.

Precision

Focus when false positives are costly (predicting Yes when it’s actually No).

Best for: Spam detection, fraud alerts, medical testing.

Recall

Focus when false negatives are costly (missing actual Yes cases).

Best for: Disease screening, churn prevention, safety monitoring.

Measure stability across folds

Compute the chosen metric for each fold. Then summarize the stability.

Report individual fold scores and, when useful, repeat cross-validation with different splits.

fold_scores = scores
mean = scores.mean()
sd = scores.std(ddof=1)
range_ = (scores.min(), scores.max())

Fold scores share overlapping training data; their spread is not a confidence interval for generalisation performance.

Report all three (recommended)

Always look at these together for the full picture.

  • Mean (μ): average performance across folds
  • Sample SD: spread of performance
  • Score distribution: individual fold or repeated-CV scores

Also check the min–max range across folds.

Interpret stability in context

There are no universal percentage bands or automatic proceed/stop rules. Consider dataset size, class balance, the metric, domain, and the impact of errors.

ReportInterpretationWhat it means
Fold scoresDistributionInspect the full spread, including unusually low scores.
MeanAverageCompare with a relevant baseline on the same splits.
SD and rangeSpreadJudge variation against the metric and consequences of errors.
Repeated CVSplit sensitivityReport scores across repeats; investigate changes across splits.
IntervalsUncertaintyState the method and account for dependence between CV scores.
Read together:

Mean, SD, range, and the score distribution describe these splits. Consistency alone does not establish useful performance or justify deployment.
