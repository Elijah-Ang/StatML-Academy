01Begin with the decision

What happens when this model is wrong?

A model can miss a positive case or raise a false alarm. These mistakes may have different costs. Define the decision and what matters before choosing a score.

Why this works · a little deeper

A metric summarizes predictions for a defined target and sample. It does not choose the objective. The lab uses 1,000 fixed constructed validation scores, with 100 positives. These teach counts and tradeoffs; they are not evidence of a deployed screening product's performance. Define the population, prediction time, action, workload and error costs.

Quick check · Why can two applications prefer different thresholds for the same model?

They may have different error costs, review capacity, prevalence or action constraints.

Next: Choose the denominator ↓
02Choose the denominator

Whose success rate are we measuring?

Accuracy looks at all cases. Precision looks only at alerts. Recall looks only at actual positives. Different bottom numbers answer different questions, so the same predictions can produce different percentages.

MetricNumerator / denominator
Accuracy(TP + TN) / all cases
PrecisionTP / (TP + FP)
RecallTP / (TP + FN)
SpecificityTN / (TN + FP)
F12TP / (2TP + FP + FN)
↳
Try it

Choose Precision or Recall in the visual. The matrix highlights the cases in its denominator.

Why this works · a little deeper

TP and TN are correct positive and negative decisions; FP is a false alert and FN a missed positive. F1 combines precision and recall using their harmonic mean, ignoring true negatives. If no cases are alerted, precision divides by zero and is undefined. For numeric prediction, MAE averages absolute errors and RMSE takes √mean(error²). Both use outcome units; RMSE emphasizes large misses. Percentage errors such as MAPE can fail near zero outcomes.

Quick check · For residuals 1 and 3, what are MAE and RMSE?

MAE = (1 + 3)/2 = 2. RMSE = √((1² + 3²)/2) = √5 ≈ 2.24. Larger misses receive more weight in RMSE.

Why this works · a little deeper

The counts are TP=80, FN=20, FP=90, TN=810. Precision=80/170≈47.1%; 90/170≈52.9% of alerts are false. Accuracy=890/1000=89%. Predicting every case negative gives 90% accuracy but zero recall. These fixed teaching counts do not include sampling or label uncertainty.

Quick check · Which denominator answers “how many of the alerts were right”?

TP + FP, the total alerts. That gives precision, not recall.

Next: Separate ranking from decisions ↓
04Separate ranking from decisions

What does a threshold change?

The fitted scores stay fixed. A cutoff turns them into yes/no decisions. ROC compares recall with false-positive rate across cutoffs. A precision–recall curve shows how well the positive predictions perform.

QuestionUseful view
Can scores rank positives above negatives?ROC curve and AUC
How reliable are alerts as recall changes?Precision–recall curve
What happens at this one threshold?Confusion matrix and workload
↳
Try it

Compare ROC above with precision–recall below. Move the threshold and follow both operating points.

Why this works · a little deeper

ROC-AUC describes positive-versus-negative ranking, giving ties half credit. It is not accuracy at one cutoff or a calibration score. Precision depends on prevalence, the actual positive fraction. The curves and matrix here use the same fixed labelled scores. With no alerts, precision is undefined, so the PR operating point is omitted rather than invented. A strictly increasing score transformation preserves rankings but may change calibration.

Quick check · If every score is changed by a strictly increasing transformation, must the ranking change?

No. Ranking can stay identical while the numerical probabilities and their calibration change.

Why this works · a little deeper

Scores equal to the cutoff count as positive here. Cost values are illustrative weights, not measured money or clinical costs. Changing a cost does not change the probabilities. Tune the decision using validation and explicitly stated consequences. A capacity limit such as 120 reviews is another possible constraint; compare it with the actual alert count.

Quick check · Does lowering the threshold improve the underlying probability estimates?

No. It changes decisions, not the scores or their calibration.

Next: Assumptions and failure modes ↓
06Assumptions and failure modes

When is a metric answering the wrong question?

A correctly calculated score can still describe the wrong sample. Check how cases were chosen, whether labels are reliable, and whether the evaluation resembles real use.

Assumptions

Independent held-out predictions; a deployment-relevant sample; valid labels; the same outcome and decision definitions.

When the method fails

Leakage, distribution shift, misleading averages, tiny subgroups, unequal error costs and metric shopping.

Try it

Raise the threshold to 1.00. See the high-accuracy all-negative baseline and its zero recall.

Why this works · a little deeper

Changing class frequency can change precision even when sensitivity and specificity stay fixed in a hypothetical comparison. Real population changes may also change those rates. Repeated people need appropriate grouped evaluation; forecasting needs time-respecting evaluation. Report subgroup results with enough data and uncertainty rather than assuming an overall score describes everybody.

Quick check · Why can 99% accuracy be useless with 1% positives?

An all-negative rule attains 99% accuracy while missing every positive case.

Next: Use an honest workflow ↓
07Use an honest workflow

When do we look at the final test?

Choose the goal and main score first. Develop the model and cutoff using validation. Lock the whole pipeline, then evaluate once on a protected final test.

  1. Define the decision and costs.
  2. Choose metrics and a relevant baseline.
  3. Fit and tune within development data.
  4. Lock preprocessing, model and threshold.
  5. Report final test estimates with uncertainty.
↳
Try it

Return to a threshold you can justify. State its trade-off before reading the scores.

Why this works · a little deeper

Preparation, selection, calibration and threshold choice are all part of the procedure. Learn them within appropriate development boundaries, including inside folds. Bootstrap uncertainty needs a resampling unit that matches the data structure. This interactive lesson remains a validation demonstration; moving a cutoff is not opening a final test. Retuning after viewing a final score makes it development feedback.

Quick check · What belongs on the final test set?

One evaluation of the locked procedure. Threshold searches and feature choices belong in development.

Next: Report probability quality too ↓
08Report probability quality too

Does “70%” behave like seventy out of a hundred?

Calibration asks whether predicted probabilities match observed frequencies. Among similar cases scored near 70%, do about 70% actually turn out positive? Good ranking does not guarantee this agreement.

Probability scoreSame fixed predictions
Brier: mean (p − y)²0.1371
Log loss: −mean log P(actual class)0.4530

What to report: definition, threshold, prevalence, baseline, sample size, uncertainty, costs and relevant subgroup results.

↳
Try it

Compare each displayed bin’s average probability with its actual positive fraction. Inspect the bin counts before judging the difference.

Why this works · a little deeper

Brier score averages (probability−binary target)²; it reflects calibration and discrimination together. Log loss strongly penalizes confident mistakes. A reliability diagram compares average prediction with observed positive frequency in bins. This lab uses five fixed-width bins and shows their counts; small bins can be noisy. Even always predicting prevalence can be calibrated while ranking poorly. Fit recalibration using development predictions, not final-test answers.

Quick check · Does a low Brier score prove every subgroup is calibrated?

No. It summarizes probability error overall. Inspect calibration, subgroup evidence and uncertainty separately.

Next: Check the reasoning ↓
09Check the reasoning

Can you explain the result without hiding behind a score?

Explain who was evaluated, what was predicted, what action followed, and what the mistakes meant. Use the checks below to support a clear decision rather than hiding behind one flattering number.

“At threshold ___, we catch ___ of the positives, send ___ alerts, and accept ___ false alarms under these stated costs.”

Missed positive cases are especially costly. Which rate directly measures how many positives we catch?

Answer and explanation

Recall. Recall divides true positives by all actual positives. Still report false positives and workload.

No predictions are positive. What is precision?

Answer and explanation

Undefined: its denominator is zero. TP + FP is zero. State the undefined value and show that there were no alerts.

What does Brier score evaluate?

Answer and explanation

Squared probability error. It averages (p − y)² and reflects calibration and discrimination; it is not a pure calibration measure.

Where should a decision threshold be chosen?

Answer and explanation

Validation predictions, or predictions made while each row was left out of fitting. Keep the final test independent of model and decision-rule selection.

What should accompany a point estimate?

Answer and explanation

A relevant baseline and uncertainty. Describe the sample, assumptions and uncertainty so the comparison can be interpreted.

Why this works · a little deeper

Accuracy, F1 and AUC each leave out part of the decision story.

Quick check · What is missing from a report that says only “Accuracy = 89%”?

The sample, prevalence, baseline, confusion counts or relevant error rates, threshold, decision costs, uncertainty and how the evaluation was kept independent.
