01The Prediction Problem

Why can fitted predictions differ from reality?

A fitted model depends on its allowed shapes and the particular training sample. A new outcome can also contain variation the inputs cannot predict. These are different sources of prediction error.

Why this works · a little deeper

For a teaching model Y=f(x)+ε, f(x) is the average outcome at input x and ε is remaining noise. A fitted model estimates f using a training sample. Real data need not separate neatly into visible signal and static. Even an excellent mean prediction cannot remove every individual outcome's variation.

02Bias: the average miss

What does a consistently rigid model miss?

Bias is a repeated average miss. Imagine fitting the method to many new training samples. If its average prediction still misses the true average outcome, it has bias there. A too-rigid shape is one possible reason.

Why this works · a little deeper

At a fixed x, bias is E[estimated f(x)]−f(x), with the average over repeated training samples. It can come from the model family, penalties, estimation procedure or data problems. A straight line applied to a curve is one illustration, not the definition of all bias.

Quick check · Would collecting more data always remove this model bias?

No. The model family may still be too restrictive.

03Variance: changing fits across samples

What makes a flexible fit unstable?

Prediction variance means the fitted answer changes across training samples. A very flexible model can change a lot when the sample changes. This is different from the spread of the raw outcomes.

Why this works · a little deeper

Variance is the spread of estimated f(x) across training samples. An unstable fit can respond strongly to small sample changes. Overfitting means learning sample-specific details that harm new-data performance. A flexible model is not guaranteed to overfit, and raw outcome variance is not the same as fitted prediction variance.

Quick check · Does a wiggly line alone prove high variance?

No. Variance concerns behavior across training samples.

04The Error Decomposition

Where does the squared-error decomposition apply?

For squared error at one fixed input, average prediction error can be split into squared bias, prediction variance and noise variance under the stated model. This particular equation is not a rule for every loss or task.

Why this works · a little deeper

Assume new zero-mean noise is independent of the training sample. For squared loss at a fixed x, expected error = squared bias + prediction variance + noise variance. These averages concern repeated fits and a new outcome. They are not calculated by relabelling the residuals of one dataset. The lab can show them because the teaching data generator's mean is known.

Quick check · Does the same additive formula automatically hold for classification accuracy?

No. The loss and assumptions matter.

06The Testing Problem

Why is training error insufficient?

A low training error describes fit to the rows already used. Resampling creates held-out comparisons from limited development data. Each fit must exclude the rows used to score it, including from learned preprocessing. Keep a separate final evaluation out of this model-selection process.

08k-Fold Cross-Validation

What does K-fold validation average?

K-fold validation splits development data into K groups. Hold one group out, fit on the others, and score the held-out rows. Repeat for every group, then average the losses. Refit learned preparation inside each training turn.

Follow the ideaRead it in plain language

Why this works · a little deeper

For five folds, fit on four and validate on the fifth, five times. Learned scaling, imputation and feature selection must be refitted inside every training fold. Average held-out losses with appropriate weighting when fold sizes differ. Overlapping fits are related, so the scores are not independent experiments. Cross-validation gives evidence under a design, not certainty about the future.

Quick check · May a scaler be fitted once on the whole dataset before CV?

No. That leaks kept-out information into the folds.

09Leave-One-Out (LOOCV)

What changes in leave-one-out?

Leave-one-out holds out one row at a time. It fits a model for each row using all the others. This uses nearly all rows for each fit, but can still be costly or give a variable estimate.

Why this works · a little deeper

With n rows, leave-one-out fits n models and averages n held-out losses. The fits overlap heavily. Its bias, variability and cost depend on the method and population; it is not always better than K-fold. The lab's five-fold selector remains a five-fold illustration, not an implementation of n leave-one-out fits.

Quick check · Does more folds always mean a better selection estimate?

No.

10The Bootstrap

What does sampling with replacement mean?

Bootstrapping samples from the original rows with replacement. Replacement means a selected row stays available for the next draw. Some rows appear several times and some not at all. Repeating this helps study an estimate's stability.

Why this works · a little deeper

From 100 rows, draw 100 times while keeping every original row available after each draw. Recalculate the statistic, then repeat. The resulting spread approximates sampling uncertainty under suitable assumptions. It is not fresh independent data and does not repair an unrepresentative sample. Related data need suitable group or time bootstrap methods.

Quick check · Is a bootstrap sample just a shuffle?

No. A shuffle preserves each row once; a bootstrap does not.

11Limitations in Practice

Which resampling method answers my question?

Choose resampling for the question. Cross-validation often checks new-data prediction. Bootstrap methods often estimate uncertainty. Time order and related groups need splitting or resampling rules that respect their structure.

Prediction assessment and estimate uncertainty are different targets. Choose resampling units that reflect independent people, groups, times or locations. 12Summary & Synthesis

Can I distinguish bias, variance, and uncertainty?

Keep the three ideas separate. Bias is the average miss. Variance is how predictions change across training samples. Uncertainty describes how sure we are about a reported quantity. They are related, but not identical.

Why this works · a little deeper

Use the definitions rather than personality metaphors about a model being stubborn or distracted. State the input, learning procedure, sample design, loss and what was averaged. Distinguish one fit's errors from repeated-sample behavior, and avoid calling every smooth curve biased or every wiggly curve wrong without evidence.
