What can we learn without a target?

Each point describes one customer by age and monthly spending. We do not have a known correct group label. K-means makes groups around centres using the distance rule we choose.

Why this works · a little deeper

These 36 customers are synthetic teaching observations. Colours name proposed clusters, not verified types of customer. Feature choice, units, distance and K all define the grouping. Raw and standardized distance are available; the axes stay in original units, so a change in the distance rule does not secretly change the customers.

No. Color identifies a proposed group. Interpretation requires domain knowledge and external evidence.

02Place the first centers

Why does the starting point matter?

Start with K centres. This lab chooses K different observed points using a reproducible seed. The seed controls the starting choice, not the customer data. Different starts can end in different groups.

Method: random observations (Forgy).

Why this works · a little deeper

This initializer uses a seeded shuffle of distinct observations. K-means++ is another method that favors separated starts using squared distances; it does not guarantee a globally best result. Random partitions are different again. Multiple restarts compare completed fits using the same data and scale. K is chosen before Lloyd's assignment/update iterations, not discovered automatically by them.

Quick check · Does changing the seed create different customers?

No. It only changes initial centers. The points remain fixed.

Why this works · a little deeper

Straight-line distance is √Σ(x_j−centre_j)². The square root can be omitted when only comparing which distance is smallest. Equal distances follow a fixed lower-centre-number rule here. In standardized mode, calculations use transformed coordinates even though the axes show original units. Screen spacing alone is not the numerical distance rule.

Quick check · Do we move a center immediately after assigning one point?

No. Assign every point using the same current centers, then update the centers as a separate phase.

Next: Recenter by averaging ↓
04Recenter by averaging

Why does the center move to the mean?

For each group, average its members' coordinates. That mean becomes the new centre. For fixed membership, it gives the smallest total squared distance within that group.

new center = (mean x, mean y)

Completed recentering rounds: 0

Try it

The outlined group and hollow cross show a worked mean. Click Assign points if needed, then Move centers. Watch the star move while memberships stay fixed.

Why this works · a little deeper

The coordinate average minimizes squared Euclidean distances for fixed group members. That is why the mean and this objective belong together. Manhattan distance would use a different objective and centre rule. An empty group has no mean; this lab retains its previous centre and reports the empty group. Animated intermediate positions illustrate movement, not extra calculated clustering steps.

Quick check · Must the new center coincide with an observed customer?

No. The average may lie between observations.

Why this works · a little deeper

Each exact assignment or mean-update phase cannot increase the squared-distance objective. It can settle at a local solution, not the globally smallest WCSS. Scrolling changes the explanatory scene without restarting the run. Changing K, scale, scenario or seed starts a new experiment. Compare completed solutions and stability rather than treating iteration count as group usefulness.

Quick check · Can two runs converge to different answers on exactly the same data?

Yes. Different initial centers can lead to different local minima.

Next: Measure within-cluster spread ↓
06Measure within-cluster spread

What is WCSS actually adding up?

WCSS is the sum of each point's squared distance to its assigned centre. It measures how compact the groups are in the chosen input scale. A smaller value is better for that particular objective.

WCSS = Σᵢ ‖xᵢ − c(assigned i)‖²

Why this works · a little deeper

WCSS is also called inertia. It depends on input units, scaling, number of rows and K. Do not compare raw-dollar WCSS with standardized WCSS as if they had the same units. The optimal objective cannot increase when K rises on fixed data and scale, but a limited restart procedure may miss those optima. Compactness alone does not establish useful or meaningful segments.

Quick check · Can we compare WCSS = 20 after standardization with WCSS = 500 in raw units?

Not as evidence that one fit is better: the distance units and therefore the objective have changed.

Next: Look for an elbow ↓

How many groups earn their complexity?

Compare several K values. See how much each added centre reduces WCSS. An elbow suggests smaller gains after that point. It is a clue to judge, not an automatic answer.

Try it

Compare the plotted K values. Choose one near a bend, then use the Lesson map to return to a grouping section and run its steps.

Why this works · a little deeper

The elbow curve is computed from the displayed data and scale. A clear elbow may not exist. Compare stability, silhouette and outside knowledge with the intended use. Silhouette compares closeness within a cluster with separation from another cluster, using the chosen metric. More groups also mean more interpretation and maintenance work; no score proves one true partition.

Quick check · If the curve bends smoothly with no obvious corner, is K-means broken?

No. There may be no uniquely compelling K. State the ambiguity and use additional evidence.

Next: Choose what distance means ↓
08Choose what distance means

Should a dollar count as much as a year?

Dollar differences can be numerically much larger than age differences. Standardization measures each input relative to its own spread. It changes what similar means; it does not prove both features are equally important.

Metric choiceWhat one unit means
Raw EuclideanOne year and one dollar enter numerically
Z-score EuclideanOne standard deviation in each feature
↳
Try it

Choose raw units or standardized units, then press Run. The same customers stay in place while the fitted groups change.

Why this works · a little deeper

A z-score is (value−mean)/SD. Standardization can be useful when relative variation should have comparable influence, but is not a universal requirement. Domain weights or common-unit raw distances may be better for the question. This exploratory lab uses all displayed rows to fit its scale; a predictive pipeline must fit scaling and centres inside training folds. Extreme values can affect the scaler too.

Quick check · Does standardizing prove that age and spending are equally important?

No. It imposes a particular distance convention. Importance is a domain question, not a consequence of unit conversion.

Next: Find the limits ↓
09Find the limits

When do neat circles tell the wrong story?

K-means prefers compact groups around means. A curved group, very different densities or extreme values can make that description unhelpful. The algorithm can finish correctly and still answer the wrong practical question.

Try two stress tests: curved moons and one unusually large spender.

What to report: features, scaling, K, initialization, restarts, final WCSS, stability and why the segments are useful.

↳
Try it

Choose Curved moons or Add outlier, then run. Describe what the method missed.

Why this works · a little deeper

Means can be pulled by extreme values. Curved, connected shapes may be divided into several compact clusters. Density-based or hierarchical methods offer different descriptions, not universal replacements. A new customer can use the saved scale and nearest-centre rule. Refitting the whole model changes what the group labels mean, so keep that distinct from assignment to a frozen model.

Quick check · Does reaching “Converged” establish that the clusters are real?

No. It only says this optimization procedure stopped changing assignments. Geometry, stability and domain meaning still need evaluation.
