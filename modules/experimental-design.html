Stage 01

The problem

Causal conclusions depend more on assignment, measurement and follow-up than on a sophisticated final test. Design defines what comparison the data can identify.

Stage 02

Intuition and objective

Random assignment balances confounders in expectation. Blocking improves balance on known prognostic variables; clustering changes the independent unit; blinding protects measurement and behaviour.

Stage 03

Worked example

Twenty schools—not 600 students—are randomised to a teaching programme. Student outcomes remain clustered within schools. Treating 600 rows as independent exaggerates precision; analysis and power must account for 20 randomisation units and intraclass correlation.

Stage 04

Reasoning with the method

An estimand specifies population, treatment strategies, comparator, outcome, time and summary contrast. Allocation concealment protects enrolment; blinding protects behaviour and measurement. Blocking randomises within prognostic strata. Cluster randomisation assigns groups and reduces effective sample size through intraclass correlation, so power and analysis must use the randomisation unit. Intention-to-treat preserves the randomised contrast; per-protocol analysis answers a different, more selection-sensitive question. Noncompliance, attrition and interference should be anticipated in the protocol. Power planning must include meaningful effect, variance, allocation, clustering, multiplicity and attrition.

A defensible analysis connects the mathematical target to the real decision. Identify what is estimated, which observations contribute independent information, what choices are learned from data, and which uncertainty remains after fitting. Then ask how the conclusion would change under another reasonable sample, a plausible alternative specification, or a shift in the deployment population. This reasoning must happen before the final test set is opened. The test result evaluates the locked procedure; it does not choose that procedure. Sensitivity analysis is most useful when it is planned around credible failure modes rather than used to search for a preferred answer.

Stage 05

Interactive laboratory

Adjust sample size, clustering and effect size. Compare naive and design-adjusted effective sample size and approximate power.

Stage 06

Assumptions and failure modes

Assumptions

Assignment is implemented as planned, outcomes are measured consistently, interference is limited or modelled, and attrition does not destroy exchangeability.

When the method fails

Noncompliance, differential attrition, spillovers, outcome switching, hidden multiplicity and analysing below the randomisation unit can bias results or uncertainty.

Stage 07

Practical workflow

  1. Define population, intervention, comparator, outcome and estimand
  2. Choose the randomisation unit
  3. Block or stratify when justified
  4. Plan sample size with clustering and attrition
  5. Pre-specify primary analysis and multiplicity
  6. Track adherence and missingness
  7. Report intention-to-treat and justified secondary analyses

Stage 08

Interpretation and reporting

What to report: Report protocol, randomisation and concealment, units, exclusions, attrition, adherence, effect with confidence interval, harms, multiplicity and deviations.

Common misuse: Randomising classrooms but analysing pupils as though all observations were independent.

Stage 09

Knowledge checks

Schools assigned to treatment means the randomisation unit is…

Answer and explanation

School. Assignment occurred at the school level.

Clustering usually makes naive standard errors…

Answer and explanation

Too small. Within-cluster similarity reduces effective independent information.

The estimand specifies…

Answer and explanation

The population-level causal contrast of interest. It defines precisely what effect the study aims to estimate.

Blocking is used to…

Answer and explanation

Improve balance and precision on known factors. Randomisation still occurs within planned blocks.

Twenty outcomes tested without a plan create…

Answer and explanation

Multiplicity risk. More tests create more chances for a small p-value.
