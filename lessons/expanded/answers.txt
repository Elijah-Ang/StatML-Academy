[anova]
Students in the same group do not all get the same score. ANOVA compares group averages. It asks whether the gaps between those averages are large compared with the spread inside the groups. | Observation~One student's score;Group mean~Average score for one method;Grand mean~Average of all students | Do different sample means prove different population means? | No. Sampling alone can produce unequal sample means.
A gap needs something to compare it with. A five-point gap stands out when most students have similar scores. It is harder to separate when scores inside each group vary a lot. | Same mean gap~5 points;Within-group SD of 2~Relatively distinct groups;Within-group SD of 20~Substantial overlap | What happens if spread increases while the gap stays fixed? | The evidence for a mean difference generally weakens.
The usual test needs separate, independent observations, roughly bell-shaped errors, and similar error spread in the groups. These are assumptions: conditions the test relies on. A neat-looking plot cannot prove the observations are independent. | Independent errors~Check assignment and sampling;Similar variances~Inspect group spreads;Unusual observations~Investigate their origin | Are three scores from the same student three independent students? | No. Repeated observations need a design-aware analysis.
Compare a student's score with the overall average. Part of that gap comes from their group's average. The rest is the student's gap from their own group average. ANOVA separates these two sources of spread. | Total SS~Between SS + within SS;Between~How far group means sit from the grand mean;Within~How far students sit from their group means | Which term grows if group means move apart? | Between-group variation grows, holding within-group variation fixed.
Divide each sum of squares by its degrees of freedom, the independent pieces left after estimating averages. That gives a mean square. F divides the between-group mean square by the within-group mean square, comparing them on a similar scale. | MS between~SS between / (k − 1);MS within~SS within / (N − k);F~MS between / MS within | If both mean squares are 12, what is F? | F = 1. The two variance estimates are equal.
Degrees of freedom count how many values can still change freely after we have used information to find averages. Once an average is fixed, the gaps from it must add to zero. Some values are therefore no longer free. | Three groups, N = 30~k = 3;Between df~3 − 1 = 2;Within df~30 − 3 = 27 | What are the total degrees of freedom here? | N − 1 = 29, which equals 2 + 27.
First divide each sum of squares by its own degrees of freedom. Then divide the between-group result by the within-group result. Keep the two sets of numbers on their correct sides of the fraction. | Between SS = 120, df = 2~MS = 60;Within SS = 270, df = 27~MS = 10;F~60 / 10 = 6 | With MS between 24 and MS within 8, what is F? | F = 3.
Read the ANOVA table one row at a time. SS is the total squared spread. df is degrees of freedom. MS is SS divided by df. F compares the between-group MS with the within-group MS. | Source~Between / within / total;Scale~SS → divide by df → MS;Test~F uses the first two mean squares | Does the total row have its own F test? | No. The omnibus F compares between and within mean squares.
A small p-value means a result at least this extreme would be unusual if all population group means were equal and the test assumptions held. It does not say the gap is big or useful. Check the size of the gap too. | p-value~Evidence under a null model;η²~SS between / SS total;Practical importance~Compare with a meaningful score difference | Does p = 0.001 imply a large effect? | No. Report effect size, uncertainty, and practical context as well.
The overall test asks whether all group averages are equal. It does not tell us which pairs differ. Compare the pairs or planned group combinations separately, allowing for the extra chances of false alarms. | Omnibus question~Are all population means equal?;Pairwise question~How much does A differ from B?;Multiplicity~Adjust the family of comparisons | Does rejecting the omnibus null mean every pair differs? | No. Some pairs may remain still consistent with equal means under the test.
A factor is a category we want to compare, such as teaching method. Adding a second factor, such as study setting, lets us check each combination. We can also ask whether a method works differently in different settings. | Factor A~Teaching method;Factor B~Study setting;Cell~One method in one setting | What is a missing combination a problem for? | It can make effects inseparable or prevent estimation of an interaction.
A marginal mean is an average across the other factor. If each combination has the same number of students, average its cell means equally. With unequal group sizes, decide whether you want equal settings or equal students to count. | Method A indoors~60;Method A outdoors~70;Equal-weight marginal mean~65 | If one setting has many more students, is every average still 65? | No. A student-weighted average differs from an equal-setting average.
An interaction means one factor's effect changes with the other factor. Compare the gaps between groups in each setting. If the gaps change, there is an interaction in those averages. The lines do not have to cross. | Indoors: B − A~8;Outdoors: B − A~2;Difference of differences~2 − 8 = −6 | Can two rising, nonparallel lines show interaction? | Yes. The change in the gap is what matters.
In a balanced two-factor example, total spread has four parts: factor A, factor B, their interaction, and the remaining errors. An interaction is a pattern in the group averages. It is not just leftover random spread. | Explained parts~A + B + A×B;Unexplained part~Residual SS;Total~Sum of all four in the balanced design | Is the interaction just another name for residual error? | No. It is a modeled systematic difference in effects.
Start with how the study was run. Then check whether effects change across settings. Report the group differences and their uncertainty, as well as the F tests. One overall average can hide a useful pattern. | First~What was randomized or sampled?;Then~Does the effect depend on context?;Finally~How large and uncertain is each contrast? | Why can one overall method effect be misleading? | An average can conceal effects that differ substantially by setting.
Choose the method for the data you actually have. Welch ANOVA allows unequal group spreads. Repeated people or groups of related people need methods that handle that link. Using ranks changes the question; it does not fix related observations. | Unequal variances~Consider Welch ANOVA;Repeated people~Repeated-measures or mixed model;Ordinal outcome~Consider an appropriate rank or ordinal model | Does switching to ranks fix dependent observations? | No. Dependence still needs to be handled.

[correlation]
Each dot must join two measurements from the same person or object. One student's hours and that same student's score make one pair. Keep the pair together when you sort or clean the data. | Student A~Hours 2, score 55;Student B~Hours 5, score 73;Pairing rule~Keep each student's values together | May I sort each column separately before computing r? | No. That creates pairs that were never observed.
Look at the dots before calculating anything. Do they rise, fall, or curve? Direction tells us which way they move. Shape tells us whether a straight-line summary is suitable. | Positive direction~Higher x tends to accompany higher y;Negative direction~Higher x tends to accompany lower y;Curved form~A straight-line summary may miss it | Can a strong U-shaped relationship have r near zero? | Yes. Pearson r summarizes linear association.
Pearson's r describes how closely the dots follow a straight-line pattern. It runs from −1 to +1. Positive means they tend to rise together; negative means one tends to fall as the other rises. It does not measure cause or prediction error. | r = +1~All points on an increasing line;r = 0~No linear association in the sample;r = −1~All points on a decreasing line | Does converting metres to centimetres change r? | No, provided the conversion is a positive linear rescaling.
The usual test starts with a model where the population's straight-line correlation is zero. Its p-value asks how unusual the result would be under that model and its assumptions. It is not the chance that the zero-correlation model is true. | Null~ρ = 0;Sample statistic~r;Common test~t = r√((n − 2)/(1 − r²)) | Does failing to reject prove independence? | No. Nonlinear dependence may remain, and the study may be imprecise.
Subtract each measurement's average. Multiply the two gaps for each pair. Matching signs give a positive product; opposite signs give a negative one. Combine and scale these products to calculate r. | Above both means~Positive product;Below both means~Positive product;Above x, below y~Negative product | What is the product for deviations +2 and −3? | −6. This pair pulls the covariance downward.
One number cannot show every shape. A strong U-shaped pattern can have r near zero because the upward and downward parts cancel. A near-zero r does not mean there is no relationship. | Linear trend~Pearson r can summarize it;Monotonic curve~Ranks may help;U-shape~Plot it and model the curve | Would Spearman correlation fully describe a symmetric U-shape? | No. Spearman also targets a relationship that keeps rising or keeps falling.
One far-away point can change r a lot. Check whether it is a real observation or an error. Compare the results with and without it, but only remove it for a sound reason. | Outlier~Unusual observed value;Influence~Large change in the result;Sensitivity~Report both justified analyses | Should a point be deleted because it lowers r? | No. Check measurement, sampling, and the scientific context.
Report r together with the plot, number of pairs, and uncertainty. A small p-value does not automatically mean a strong relationship. A large dataset can detect a weak straight-line pattern. | Estimate~r and its direction;Uncertainty~An appropriate interval or test;Context~Sampling, shape, and unusual points | Can a weak correlation be statistically detectable in a large sample? | Yes. Detectability does not establish practical importance.
Two things can move together because one affects the other, because both share another cause, or because of how the data was selected. Correlation alone cannot tell these explanations apart. | Shared cause~Hot weather raises ice-cream sales and swimming;Observed association~Sales and drownings can rise together;Causal conclusion~Needs a credible design and assumptions | Does controlling one variable automatically remove all confounding? | No. Important unmeasured or misspecified causes may remain.
Choose the measure for the pattern. Pearson uses the numerical values to describe a straight-line relationship. Spearman and Kendall use order or ranks. A rank is a value's position when the values are sorted. | Pearson~Linear association of numerical values;Spearman~Association of ranks;Kendall~Agreement of pairwise ordering | Which measure suits ordered categories and a pattern that keeps rising? | A rank-based measure such as Spearman or Kendall may suit that question. Check the plot and measurement meaning too.

[chi-square]
Use counts when the values are categories, such as club and favourite snack. Put each person in the matching table cell. Chi-square compares those real counts with counts expected if the two categories were unrelated. | Row variable~Treatment A or B;Column variable~Recovered or not;Cell~Number of people in one combination | Should percentages replace counts in the chi-square calculation? | No. Use counts; percentages can help interpretation.
Independence means knowing the row category would not change the outcome proportions in the population. The expected table keeps the same row totals and column totals. It spreads the outcomes across rows at the overall rates. | Overall recovery~60 of 100 people;A group size~40 people;Expected A recoveries~40 × 0.60 = 24 | Does independence require equal row sizes? | No. Expected counts account for unequal margins.
Take the row's size and multiply it by the overall fraction in the column. That is the expected count. For 40 people in a row and 60% overall recovery, we expect 40 × 0.60 = 24 recoveries. | Row total~40;Column proportion~60 / 100;Expected count~40 × 60 / 100 = 24 | With row total 50 and column share 30%, what is expected? | 15 observations.
For each cell, subtract expected from observed. Multiply the gap by itself, then divide by expected. Add all the cell results. Squaring stops positive and negative gaps cancelling each other. | Observed~30;Expected~24;Contribution~(30 − 24)² / 24 = 1.5 | Can a cell contribution be negative? | No. Every squared contribution is nonnegative.
The p-value is the area to the right of our statistic on the reference curve. It tells us how often a statistic at least this large would occur under independence and the test assumptions. It is not the chance that independence is true. | Degrees of freedom~(rows − 1)(columns − 1);For a 2×2 table~1;Tail area~Probability of a statistic at least this large under H₀ | Is the p-value the chance the variables are independent? | No. Independence is assumed when computing the tail probability.
A small p-value gives evidence against independence under the test model. It does not tell us the cause or how large the link is. Look at cell proportions and an association-size measure too. | Test~Evidence against independence;Cramér's V~Strength of association;Cell pattern~Where observed and expected differ | Does chi-square tell us which variable causes the other? | No. Causality depends on design and additional assumptions.
Tiny expected counts can make the usual approximation unreliable. Related people, repeat visits and survey weights can also require a different method. Choose a test that matches how the data was collected. | Sparse 2×2 counts~Consider an exact test;Paired binary responses~Consider a paired method;Weighted or clustered survey~Use design-aware inference | Are 100 visits by ten people 100 independent people? | No. The repeated observations violate the usual assumption that these observations are independent.

[probability-sampling]
The population is the whole group we want to describe. Its mean is fixed once that group is defined. A sample is the smaller group we measure. Its mean can change when we choose different people. | Population~All eligible units;Parameter~Their true mean;Statistic~The mean of one sample | Which changes when you draw another sample: parameter or statistic? | The statistic changes; the defined population parameter stays fixed.
Probability describes chance. Frequency describes what happened in our sample. For separate possible values, add their probabilities. For a continuous curve, use the area over a range; the curve's height at one point is not a probability. | Discrete mass~P(X = 2);Continuous probability~P(1 < X < 2);Observed frequency~Count / sample size | Can a density curve be taller than one? | Yes. Its total area must be one; its height need not be.
Standard deviation, SD, describes how much individual values vary. Standard error, SE, describes how much an estimate varies across samples. For an independent sample mean, estimate SE by dividing sample SD by the square root of sample size. | Sample SD~12 points;Sample size~36 people;Estimated SE~12 / 6 = 2 points | What happens to SE when n quadruples, with SD fixed? | It halves.
Draw one sample and calculate its average. Place one dot for that average on another plot. Repeat. This new plot is a sampling distribution: its dots are sample averages, not individual people. | One population~Source of observations;One sample~n observations;One dot~That sample's mean | Does a histogram of 200 sample means represent 200 people? | No. Each mean summarizes a separate sample.
Bigger independent samples usually give steadier averages. But the improvement slows down. To halve the standard error, we need about four times as many observations under the same conditions. | n = 25, SD = 10~SE = 2;n = 100, SD = 10~SE = 1;Held constant~Population and sampling design | Does increasing the number of simulation repeats reduce the SE of each mean? | No. It estimates the sampling distribution more precisely.
The usual SE formula needs independent information. Related observations can overlap in what they tell us. Very extreme values can also make small-sample approximations unreliable. More rows do not always mean equally more information. | Independent people~Each contributes separate information;Repeated classmates~Some information overlaps;Consequence~Use the actual sampling design | Can a thousand correlated rows act like a thousand independent rows? | No. Their effective information can be much smaller.
Choose the group you want to describe and how people enter the sample. Random sampling helps describe that group. Random treatment assignment helps compare causes and effects. They solve different problems. | Sampling frame~Who could be selected?;Selection rule~How were units chosen?;Estimator~How do weights and design enter? | Does random treatment assignment make a convenience sample representative? | No. It does not change how participants entered the study.
Report the average, its uncertainty, sample size, and how the sample was chosen. Say who the result applies to. A very precise answer from the wrong group can still be misleading. | Estimate~Mean in outcome units;Uncertainty~SE or interval with method;Scope~Population, coverage, and limitations | Can a tiny SE rule out selection bias? | No. SE describes sampling variability under the assumed design.
Ask what the spread describes. Is it the spread of individual values? Use SD. Is it the spread of sample averages across repeated samples? Use SE. | Individual spread~SD;Mean-estimate spread~SE;Example: SD 15, n 25~SE = 3 | For SD = 8 and n = 16, what is SE? | 8 / √16 = 2, under independent sampling.

[confidence-hypothesis-testing]
An estimate is one answer from the data. An interval adds a range showing the uncertainty of that answer under a chosen method. A narrower interval means more precision. It does not mean the effect is larger. | Estimated effect~4 points;Study A SE~1 point;Study B SE~3 points | Which study gives the narrower interval for the same estimate? | Study A, because its standard error is smaller.
A confidence interval is made by a method designed to cover the fixed population value at a stated rate across repeated samples. A p-value asks how unusual the data would be under a chosen null model. Neither gives the chance that the null model is true. | 95% confidence~95% coverage over repeated applications;Small p~Observed statistic unusual under H₀;Not supplied~Probability that H₀ is true | Does a 95% interval assign 95% probability to this fixed parameter? | Not in the usual frequentist interpretation.
For independent normal measurements with known population SD, SE = SD/√n. A 95% interval is estimate ± 1.96×SE. Compare the estimate with the null value using z = (estimate − null)/SE. If SD is estimated, a t reference is usually needed instead. | Estimate~4;Known σ = 10, n = 100~SE = 1;95% interval~4 ± 1.96 = [2.04, 5.96] | What is z when the null is zero in this example? | z = 4 / 1 = 4.
Alpha is the planned false-alarm rate when the null model is true. Power is the chance of detecting a particular real effect under the planned method. Bigger samples, bigger effects and less noise usually make detection easier. | True null rejected~Type I error;Specified alternative missed~Type II error;Power~1 − β | Is power the probability that an observed result is true? | No. Power is a planned chance of detecting one specified effect.
With the same spread, a bigger independent sample gives smaller SE and a narrower interval. It can make a tiny effect easier to detect. The effect itself has not become more useful just because the interval narrowed. | n = 25, σ = 10~SE = 2;n = 100, σ = 10~SE = 1;Practical threshold~Must come from the application | Can an enormous study detect a trivial effect? | Yes. Statistical precision and practical importance are separate.
Related observations, many tests, unplanned stopping and a poor model can break the promised error rate. Deciding the analysis after seeing the answer also changes what the reported p-value means. | Dependence~Rows share information;Multiplicity~Many opportunities for a small p;Optional stopping~Repeated unplanned decisions to stop | Does correcting for many tests fix wrongly treating related rows as independent? | No. Multiple testing and related observations are different problems. Each needs its own suitable response.
Before reading the results, choose the question, outcome, meaningful effect, sampling or assignment plan, and analysis. Writing these choices down first is called prespecification. It makes the later result easier to interpret honestly. | Question~What effect and population?;Design~What observations are independent?;Analysis~Which tests and correction family? | Should the final hypothesis be whichever outcome looks most significant? | No. That is outcome selection and needs explicit exploratory treatment.
Report the estimate and interval, as well as the test method and p-value. If a test does not reject zero, the evidence was not enough for that test. It does not prove the effect is exactly zero. | Effect~4 points;Interval~Shows precision and plausible magnitudes;Conclusion~Compare with a meaningful effect threshold | Can a wide interval including zero prove equivalence? | No. Equivalence needs a planned margin and an appropriate analysis.
Read the size and uncertainty together. An estimate can be small and precise, large and uncertain, or too uncertain to decide. A yes/no significance label leaves out these differences. | Estimate~0.2 units;95% interval~[0.1, 0.3];Meaningful effect~2 units | Is this precise small estimate necessarily important? | No. It is far below the stated practical threshold.

[time-series-analysis]
A time series is a list of measurements in time order. Nearby measurements may be related. When checking a forecast, only use information that was available when that forecast would have been made. | Ordinary row label~May be interchangeable;Timestamp~Determines available history;Forecast target~A later value at a stated horizon | Is a random row split always safe for forecasting? | No. It can allow future information into training.
Plot the values against time first. Look for a long-term rise or fall, repeating seasons, unusual events and changing spread. These clues help choose a model. They do not promise that the pattern will continue. | Trend~Longer-term movement;Seasonality~Pattern repeating at a known period;Break~Change in the data-generating process | Is every repeated-looking wiggle a seasonal pattern? | No. Check repeated cycles and the underlying process.
One simple description is observed value = trend + seasonal pattern + remainder. Trend is the long-term movement. Seasonality is a repeating pattern. The remainder is what those parts leave behind. Here we know the parts because the example is simulated. | Trend at one time~20;Seasonal contribution~+3;Remainder −1~Observed value 22 | Must seasonal amplitude stay fixed in every time-series model? | No. A multiplicative or transformed model can represent changing amplitude.
A weakly stationary process has a stable average and finite, stable variance. The relationship between observations depends on their time gap rather than their dates. A flat-looking plot alone cannot prove this. | Mean~Stable over time;Variance~Stable and finite;Dependence~A function of lag | Does removing a trend automatically establish stationarity? | No. Variance, seasonality, or dependence may still change.
First differencing means subtracting the previous value. Seasonal differencing means subtracting the value one full season earlier. This can remove some changes, but it is not a cure for every pattern. | Values~20, 23, 22;First differences~3, −1;Monthly seasonal difference~yₜ − yₜ₋₁₂ | How many first differences come from five observations? | Four.
A lag is a time gap. Autocorrelation compares a series with an earlier copy of itself. For monthly data, lag 12 compares a month with the same month a year earlier. | Lag 1~Previous month;Lag 12~Previous year's same month;ACF~Correlation-like dependence summary | Does a high lag-12 ACF by itself prove a causal annual mechanism? | No. It is a descriptive pattern requiring context.
Check forecasts using only the past available at each starting time. Compare them with a simple forecast, such as repeating the last value. Check the number of steps ahead that actually matters for the decision. | Train~Earlier observations;Validate~Later forecast origins;Baseline~Last value or last season | May a twelve-step forecast use the actual value from step eleven? | Not for a fixed-origin twelve-step evaluation; that would change the task.
A forecast residual is actual value minus predicted value. Look for remaining trends, waves, changing spread or an average error away from zero. A model may have missed something predictable. | Mean residual~Systematic over- or underprediction;Residual ACF~Remaining temporal structure;Spread~Whether uncertainty changes | Must every sample residual autocorrelation be exactly zero? | No. Small random values occur even under an adequate model.
Report how far ahead you forecast, when each forecast started, what you compared it with, and how you measured errors. Explain the uncertainty method and its assumptions about future conditions. | Horizon~How far ahead?;Evaluation~Which chronological origins?;Uncertainty~How was the interval constructed? | Does a good one-step score guarantee good annual forecasts? | No. Different horizons are different prediction tasks.
The process can change. New policies, habits, sensors or seasons may make old patterns less useful. Watch both inputs and prediction errors. Investigate a change before assuming retraining is the answer. | Break~Persistent level or relationship change;Drift~Distribution changes over time;Response~Investigate, validate, then update | Does input drift alone prove prediction failure? | No. It is a warning to examine outcomes and model performance.

[multiple-linear-regression]
One clue may not be enough to predict an outcome. Multiple regression combines several inputs, called predictors. Each row still has one outcome. The model adds up the contributions from all its predictors. | Inputs~Study hours and prior score;Target~Exam score;Model~One weighted sum | Does adding a predictor guarantee better new-data predictions? | No. It can add noise or overfitting.
Start with the intercept, the prediction when all inputs are zero. Multiply each input by its coefficient, then add the products. The result is the model's predicted average outcome for those inputs. | Intercept~20;2 × hours, hours = 4~8;0.5 × prior score, prior = 60~30;Prediction~58 | What happens if only hours increase by one? | The prediction rises by 2, holding the other input fixed.
A coefficient controls how much its input changes the prediction. Changing it tilts the fitted surface. A slice shows one comparison while keeping another input fixed; it is not the entire model. | Coefficient β₁~Change per unit of x₁;Coefficient β₂~Change per unit of x₂;Slice~Fix x₂ and vary x₁ | Does a slice show every observation at its own second input? | No. Observations can sit away from a fixed-input slice.
Least squares chooses all coefficients together to make squared prediction gaps as small as possible. To get one unique unpenalized coefficient answer, the input columns must not exactly repeat combinations of one another. | Residual~Observed − predicted;Objective~Sum of squared residuals;Identifiability~No exact redundant predictor column | Can two identical columns have uniquely determined separate coefficients? | No. Their combined effect can be identified, but their separate weights cannot.
Holding constant means keeping the other input numbers fixed inside the model. Change one input and see how the prediction changes. It does not literally freeze the world or prove a cause-and-effect relationship. | Compare~x₁ = 3 versus x₁ = 4;Keep fixed~Same x₂ value;Interpretation~Adjusted model association | If two predictors nearly always move together, is this comparison well supported? | It may rely on sparse or nonexistent combinations.
Read a prediction as a running total. Add the intercept, then each input-times-coefficient contribution. A negative contribution lowers the total. Keep the input and outcome units clear. | Starting value~10;Hours contribution~3 × 4 = 12;Experience contribution~2 × 5 = 10;Total~32 | If experience becomes 6, what is the new prediction? | 34, with the other input fixed.
A relationship seen with one input alone can change when another input is included. The two inputs may share information. This adjusted relationship belongs to the fitted model; it does not automatically settle what causes what. | Unadjusted~Outcome related to x₁;Adjusted~Compare x₁ at a fixed x₂;Caution~Adjustment can also introduce bias | Should every available variable be adjusted for in causal work? | No. Mediators, colliders, and post-outcome variables need careful treatment.
Adding inputs to an ordinary least-squares fit cannot lower training R² on the same rows. Even a useless input can get credit for chance patterns. Adjusted R² adds a penalty. Validation asks whether predictions improve on other rows. | Training R²~In-sample fit;Adjusted R²~Fit with a parameter penalty;Validation error~Prediction on unused development rows | Is the largest training R² a sufficient selection rule? | No. It rewards unnecessary flexibility.
Read each coefficient with its units and uncertainty. Then check assumptions, prediction errors and validation results. Closely overlapping inputs can make individual coefficients uncertain even when combined predictions remain useful. | Coefficients~Adjusted associations;Intervals~Uncertainty under the model;Validation~Prediction beyond training rows | Does a small coefficient p-value establish causation? | No. The study design and causal assumptions remain necessary.

[polynomial-regression]
A straight line can miss a curved pattern. Polynomial regression adds powers of the input so the predicted average can bend. It still combines its coefficients by multiplying and adding. | Linear term~x;Quadratic term~x²;Possible pattern~A U-shaped mean | Is quadratic regression linear in its coefficients? | Yes. The unknown coefficients still enter as a weighted sum.
Start with a simple comparison: predict one constant average or use a straight line. This is the baseline. A more flexible curve has to improve useful predictions beyond that baseline. | Constant baseline~Training mean;Linear baseline~Intercept + slope × x;Fair comparison~Same kept-out rows | Is a complicated curve useful if validation error rises? | Usually not for this predictive task.
Make extra columns from the same input, such as x² and x³. x² means x×x; x³ means x×x×x. These columns allow new shapes. They do not give us extra observed rows. | x = 2~Original feature;x² = 4~Squared feature;x³ = 8~Cubed feature | Does creating five powers multiply the sample size by five? | No. The number of observations is unchanged.
Each coefficient controls one term in the sum. Together, the terms make the curve. Do not read one polynomial coefficient as the whole effect of x; the terms work together. | b₀~Vertical level;b₁x~Linear contribution;b₂x²~Quadratic contribution | Should a quadratic coefficient be interpreted without the other terms? | No. Inspect the combined curve and its domain.
Residuals are actual values minus the curve's predictions. A repeated bend in those errors can mean the curve missed a pattern. A widening funnel can mean the error spread changes. | Residual~y − ŷ;Curve in residuals~Possible missing nonlinear structure;Funnel~Possible nonconstant variance | Does random-looking residual scatter prove causal validity? | No. It checks only some aspects of the fitted model.
A flexible curve can follow noise in the training points. It may then predict badly on other points, especially near or beyond the edges. Use validation data to check whether the extra bends help. | More flexibility~Lower possible training error;Risk~Unstable fitted shape;Check~Held-out development error | Why is extrapolation especially risky for high-degree polynomials? | Powers can grow rapidly outside the observed range.
The degree is the highest power of x included. Compare several degrees using training and validation, or cross-validation. Choose the degree before opening the final-test results. | Train~Estimate coefficients;Validation~Choose degree and other settings;Final test~Evaluate the locked procedure | May the final test select the best degree? | No. That would turn it into another validation set.
Lock the input preparation, degree, fitting rule, penalties and error measure before final evaluation. The test evaluates that whole procedure. A curve that fits the past still needs evidence for its intended use. | Locked choice~Degree and preprocessing;Locked fit~The selected model;Next evidence~A fresh final evaluation | If the final result prompts a model revision, what is needed next? | New independent evaluation, or an explicitly exploratory interpretation.

[regression-diagnostics]
R² describes how much the fitted model reduced squared spread on a dataset. It does not certify that the model or its assumptions are suitable. Models with similar R² can miss very different patterns. | High R²~Much training variation explained;Not guaranteed~Correct mean form or independent errors;First check~Plot residuals | Can high R² coexist with a curved residual pattern? | Yes. The model can fit broadly while missing systematic structure.
A residual is actual minus predicted. Link the same row in the data plot and the residual plot. This shows where the number came from, including whether the prediction was too high or too low. | Observed y~12;Fitted ŷ~9;Residual~+3 | What does a negative residual mean? | The model predicted too high.
An outlier has an unusually large prediction gap. High leverage means unusual input values. Influence means a point changes the fit a lot. A point can have one of these properties without all three. | Outlier~Far vertically from the fit;Leverage~Unusual input location;Influence~Large change after removal | Should every high-leverage point be deleted? | No. Investigate and report a justified sensitivity analysis.
Choose the plot for the question. Residual plots show missed shape and spread. Q–Q plots compare error distributions. Leverage and VIF check different input issues. No single picture checks everything. | Residual/fitted~Mean form and spread;Q–Q~Error-distribution compatibility;VIF~Predictor redundancy | Must predictors themselves be normal for ordinary least squares? | No. Normal-error assumptions concern some forms of inference, not predictor normality.
A curve suggests the model missed a mean pattern. A funnel suggests that error spread changes. Treat these as clues. Investigate and validate a repair instead of transforming data automatically. | Curve~Consider a nonlinear mean;Funnel~Consider variance modeling or robust inference;After repair~Recheck and validate | Does every funnel require a logarithm? | No. The response depends on the outcome scale and scientific model.
A plain-looking residual plot cannot prove that observations are independent. It cannot prove a causal effect either. [imbalanced-classification]
If one in a hundred cases is positive, predicting negative for everybody gets 99% accuracy. It also misses every positive. Start with which cases matter and what each type of mistake costs. | TP~0;FN~1;TN~99;Recall~0% | Does 99% accuracy guarantee useful rare-case detection? | No.
Prevalence is the fraction of cases that are actually positive. Precision compares true positives with false alarms among alerts. With the same sensitivity and specificity, rarer positives usually mean lower precision. | Sensitivity~TP / actual positives;Specificity~TN / actual negatives;Precision~TP / all alerts | Can precision change without changing sensitivity or specificity? | Yes, when prevalence changes.
Out of 10,000 cases at 1% prevalence, 100 are positive and 9,900 negative. Finding 90% of positives gives 90 true alerts. Falsely alerting 5% of negatives gives 495 false alerts. | TP / FN~90 / 10;FP / TN~495 / 9,405;Alerts~585;Precision~90 / 585 ≈ 15.4% | Are 90% of the alerts true positives? | No. Sensitivity is 90%, but precision is about 15.4%.
Look at both rates and actual counts. A good ranking score does not tell us how many alerts people can review. The costs and capacity determine which tradeoffs matter. | Recall~Fraction of cases found;Precision~Fraction of alerts that are cases;Workload~TP + FP;Cost~False alerts and missed cases | Is ROC-AUC alone an operating policy? | No.
Lowering the cutoff adds alerts from the same fixed scores. Recall cannot decrease, but false alerts and workload can grow. Precision may change; it is not guaranteed to move in one direction at every step. | Lower threshold~Larger alerted set;Benefit~Fewer missed positives;Cost~Potentially more false alerts | Does the fitted probability model change when only threshold changes? | No.
Resample training data only. Making an evaluation set artificially balanced changes the positive frequency. Its precision may then look very different from the precision in real use. | Training resampling~A modeling choice;Evaluation prevalence~Match the target setting;Probability output~May need recalibration | May synthetic rows be created before the train-validation split? | No.
Use validation data to choose a cutoff based on stated costs or review capacity. Then lock the model and decision policy before final evaluation. | Capacity~Maximum alerts;Costs~Consequences of each error;Selection~A feasible validation operating point | Should the final test be repeatedly searched for a better threshold? | No.
Report the positive frequency, confusion counts, cutoff and alert workload. Add useful scores, probability checks and uncertainty. The reader needs enough context to understand what the decision actually does. | Population~Who is screened?;Action~What follows an alert?;Evidence~Counts and uncertainty | Can precision be compared across different prevalences without qualification? | Not safely.
For a new positive frequency, recalculate counts first. Then calculate percentages from the right groups. Precision divides by all alerts, not by all actual positives. | 1,000 people, 10% prevalence~100 positives;Sensitivity 80%, specificity 90%~TP 80, FP 90;Precision~80 / 170 ≈ 47.1% | What is the total alert workload? | 170 alerts.

[experimental-design]
First define the cause-and-effect question. Name the population, treatment, comparison, outcome, time period and effect to summarize. This exact target is called the estimand. | Population~Eligible students;Comparison~New versus usual teaching;Outcome and time~Exam score after one term | Does a p-value specify the effect we want to estimate? | No. The estimand specifies the target comparison. A p-value is one test result about a model.
Random assignment chooses treatment by chance. Across repeated assignments it balances other factors on average, but one actual study may still be unbalanced. Random sampling instead concerns who enters the study. | Assignment~Who receives which treatment?;Sampling~Who enters the study?;One allocation~Can show chance imbalance | Does random assignment guarantee identical baseline means? | No.
If twenty schools are assigned, the school is the assignment unit. Students in one school share a treatment and may have related outcomes. Six hundred student rows do not mean six hundred independent assignments. | Schools~20 randomized units;Students~600 measured outcomes;Analysis~Respect within-school dependence | What is the randomized unit in this example? | The school.
Blocking groups similar participants before random assignment. Blinding hides assignment from people whose behavior or measurements might change. Intention-to-treat compares the groups as assigned. These methods solve different problems. | Blocking~Randomize within strata;Blinding~Hide assignment where appropriate;Intention-to-treat~Retain assigned groups | Does intention-to-treat mean discarding noncompliant participants? | No.
Related people in one group may carry overlapping information. For equal group sizes, a simple design-effect formula estimates how much variance is increased. Its effective sample size is a rough comparison, not a complete power calculation. | Cluster size m~30;ICC~0.05;Design effect~2.45;n = 600~Effective n ≈ 245 | Is effective n a substitute for a full cluster-trial power calculation? | No.
People may drop out, not follow the assigned treatment, or affect other groups. Outcomes or measurement may also change. Plan for these possibilities and explain how they affect the question we can answer. | Attrition~Missing outcomes differ by group;Spillover~One unit's treatment affects another;Outcome switching~Changes the planned question | Does randomization automatically repair differential follow-up loss? | No.
Plan the target effect, assignment unit, allocation, outcomes, analysis and missing-data rules before recruitment. Choose a meaningful effect for sample-size planning and check whether the assumptions are plausible. | Before data~Meaningful effect and variability;Design~Clusters, blocking, allocation;Analysis~Multiplicity and missing-data strategy | Should the primary outcome be selected after inspecting treatment effects? | No.
Report what actually happened: assignments, exclusions, adherence, missing outcomes and any changed plans. Give effect estimates with intervals and relevant harms. The reader should be able to see the real comparison. | Who entered~Eligibility and recruitment;Who remained~Follow-up and exclusions;What happened~Effect, uncertainty, and harms | Why disclose deviations from the planned analysis? | They affect how confirmatory the interpretation can be.
Identify what was randomized and which measurements remain related. Choose the analysis and uncertainty method to respect both. Counting rows alone is not enough. | Clinics randomized~Clinic-level assignment;Patients measured~Nested outcomes;Implication~Account for within-clinic dependence | Does analyzing more patient rows create more randomized clinics? | No.

[neural-networks]
A neural network combines small calculations. It multiplies inputs by weights, adds starting values, and applies activation functions. Training changes the weights and starting values to reduce prediction loss. This lab uses a small network whose calculations can be inspected. | Input~Two numerical features;Model~2 → 3 → 1 network;Target~A binary label | Are the drawn connections decorative only? | No. Each represents a weight used in the displayed calculation.
Each training row has input measurements and a target answer. Inputs go into the network. The target helps measure training error. A new prediction must be made without knowing its target. | Inputs~x and z;Target~0 or 1;Batch~Several training rows processed together | May the target be placed among the model's input features? | No. That would reveal the answer.
Split the data before learning preparation. Fit scales or category mappings on training rows. Reuse those saved values for validation and test. Related people, images or times may need special split rules. | First~Split rows, groups, or time;Then~Learn training transforms;Finally~Apply the saved transforms | Why is “scale everything, then split” unsafe? | Held-out observations influence the fitted preprocessing.
Each neuron multiplies its inputs by weights, adds a bias, then applies an activation. A bias is a starting value. Different neurons can react to different combinations of the same inputs. | Inputs~x₁ and x₂;Preactivation~w₁x₁ + w₂x₂ + b;Output~activation(preactivation) | Does every neuron in a layer need identical weights? | No.
Every connection has its own weight. A weight is the multiplier on that connection. A bias shifts the neuron's sum. Change one weight and follow the effect through the later calculations. | x₁ = 2, w₁ = 0.5~Contribution 1;x₂ = 1, w₂ = −0.2~Contribution −0.2;Bias 0.1~Preactivation 0.9 | Which contribution changes if only w₁ changes? | The term w₁x₁, followed by its downstream effects.
An activation can bend the response. Without a bend between layers, stacked weighted sums collapse into one weighted-sum rule. Bending lets the network represent more complex relationships than one straight rule. | ReLU~max(0, z);Sigmoid~Maps to (0, 1);Tanh~Maps to (−1, 1) | Is three linear layers automatically more expressive than one linear map? | No.
A forward pass calculates the hidden sums, activations, output score and probability in order. It uses the current weights. It does not change them. | Phase 1~Hidden weighted sums;Phase 2~Hidden activations;Phase 3~Output score and probability | Does showing a prediction mean training has updated the model? | No.
Loss turns the quality of a prediction into a number to minimize. Binary cross-entropy gives a large penalty to a very confident wrong probability. A correct label alone does not tell the whole probability story. | True label~1;p = 0.8~Loss −log(0.8) ≈ 0.223;p = 0.2~Loss −log(0.2) ≈ 1.609 | Is accuracy smooth enough to be the usual gradient-training objective? | No. Cross-entropy supplies useful differentiable feedback.
Backpropagation calculates gradients: how a small change in each weight or bias would change loss. It works backwards through the calculation. It does not itself choose the step size or update the weights. | Output error signal~p − y for sigmoid plus cross-entropy;Hidden gradient~Chain of local derivatives;Result~One derivative per parameter | Must the weights change while gradients are being calculated? | No.
Gradient descent updates a parameter by subtracting learning rate times its gradient. The learning rate is the step size. A step that is too large can make the loss worse instead of better. | Old weight~0.4;Gradient~0.3;Learning rate 0.1~New weight 0.37 | What happens with a negative gradient? | Subtracting it increases the parameter.
Use one unchanged weight snapshot for the forward pass, loss and gradients. Only then apply the update. Mixing old and newly updated weights changes the intended calculation. | 1~Forward;2~Loss;3~Gradients;4~Update | May a hidden gradient use an output weight that was already updated mid-pass? | Not for the simultaneous gradient update shown here.
Training loss shows progress on training examples. Validation loss checks other examples during development. Use it to choose a checkpoint or stopping rule. Keep the final test out of that choice. | Training~Parameters receive gradients;Validation~No gradient updates;Early stopping~A development selection rule | Does a falling training loss guarantee falling validation loss? | No.
People choose the architecture, activations, learning rate, penalties and stopping rules. These settings are hyperparameters. Training learns the numerical weights and biases within that chosen setup. | Parameters~Learned weights and biases;Hyperparameters~Learning rate and architecture;Selection~Validation or cross-validation | Is a learned weight a hyperparameter? | No.
Final evaluation checks the whole frozen procedure: preparation, network, checkpoint and decision rule. This lab shows validation while you explore. The final-test labels stay sealed. | Freeze~Transforms and weights;Freeze~Threshold or action rule;Evaluate~Independent final observations | What is needed after revising the model based on test results? | Fresh independent evaluation or an explicitly exploratory claim.
Inspect particular errors as well as averages. A mistake can reveal poor data, a weak pattern or a shortcut. One example alone cannot establish performance for the whole population. | Row receipt~Inputs, target, probability, loss;Group pattern~Who is disproportionately missed?;Response~Investigate and validate | Should one surprising error automatically trigger deletion? | No.
Save more than the weights. New inputs must use the same feature order, units, category mapping and preparation. A correct network can still give nonsense when its input contract changes. | Input contract~Names, order, units, shape;Transform state~Fitted means and encodings;Model state~Architecture and parameters | Can swapping input columns change predictions with identical weights? | Yes.
Choose architecture for the information. A CNN reuses local filters, an RNN carries a state through a sequence, and attention combines values using learned relevance weights. Inspect one mechanism at a time. | CNN~Local shared filters;RNN~Sequential state;Attention~Weighted contextual information | Is one architecture best for every data type? | No.
Move an input and trace the prediction through the network. Predict the effect before checking. Then compare before and after training on the same example. | Hidden unit~Weighted sum then activation;Output score~Weighted hidden activations plus bias;Probability~Sigmoid of output score | Can you determine the prediction from the drawing's color alone? | No. Inspect the actual numerical calculation.
Keep the learning cycle in order: forward prediction, loss, gradients, update, then the next prediction. Training teaches the parameters. Validation guides settings. A separate final test checks the completed choice. | Forward~What did we predict?;Loss~How costly was that prediction?;Backward~Which changes affect loss?;Update~Apply a controlled change | Which phase actually changes parameters? | The optimizer update.

[deep-learning]
Decide what answer the system must return. Classification gives a class decision or probabilities. Detection adds locations. Segmentation labels pixels. Each task needs different answers in the training data and different checks. | Classification~One label distribution;Detection~Boxes and labels;Segmentation~A label at each pixel | Is a class probability enough to locate every object? | No.
A model may use an easy shortcut, such as background or watermark, instead of the pattern we wanted. That can work in training and fail elsewhere. Look for clues that accidentally reveal labels. | Intended clue~Task-relevant structure;Shortcut~Accidental collection signal;Check~Evaluate under realistic separation | Does excellent random-split accuracy rule out a site shortcut? | No.
Training changes the parameters. Validation guides architecture and stopping. The test checks the frozen procedure. Related images, people or time periods need split rules that keep inappropriate information from crossing. | Train~Gradient updates;Validation~Hyperparameter choices;Test~Independent final evaluation | May augmented versions of the same image cross the split? | No. Split original units before augmentation.
A tensor is an array of numbers with a stated shape. Its dimensions can mean batch, height, width and channels. Shape describes how the numbers are organized, not how similar the examples are. | RGB image~Height × width × 3;Batch of eight images~8 × height × width × 3;Contract~State dimension order | Are RGB values three separate spatial coordinates? | No. They are channels at each pixel.
One neuron multiplies inputs by weights, adds a bias, and applies an activation. Larger networks repeat and combine that small operation. The calculation is the building block, not a magical decision. | Inputs~2 and 1;Weights~0.5 and −0.2;Bias 0.1~Sum 0.9 before activation | Does a positive weight always make the final prediction larger? | Not necessarily. Downstream weights and activations matter.
Depth means more layers of transformations. Earlier responses become inputs for later ones. This can build useful representations, but does not guarantee neurons learn concepts with human names such as ears or eyes. | Early transformation~Local or simple combinations;Later transformation~Compositions of previous outputs;Task signal~Shapes what is useful | Must every hidden unit correspond to a recognizable object part? | No.
Training follows four steps: predict forward, calculate loss, calculate gradients, then update parameters. Keep the parameters unchanged until the gradients for that step are ready. | Forward~Prediction;Loss~Objective value;Backward~Parameter derivatives;Update~New parameters | Is backpropagation itself the learning-rate rule? | No. It computes gradients used by an optimizer.
Different ways to reduce overfitting change different things. Dropout hides some activations during training. Augmentation varies examples. Weight decay penalizes weights. Early stopping selects an earlier checkpoint. | Dropout~Training masks;Augmentation~Plausible input variation;Weight decay~Parameter penalty;Early stopping~Validation checkpoint | Is dropout normally applied as a random mask at ordinary evaluation time? | No. Use the corresponding deterministic evaluation convention.
Match architecture to the information. A CNN computes local filter responses. An RNN updates a state through time. Attention uses normalized weights to combine information. Follow one actual calculation in each example. | CNN~Shared local computation;RNN~State carried through sequence;Attention~Context-weighted combinations | Does attention weight alone prove causal importance? | No.
Transfer learning reuses parameters learned on an earlier task. We can freeze a backbone and train a new output head, then optionally adjust selected layers. The earlier task may still be a poor match for the new one. | First~Load pretrained representation;Then~Train a task-specific head;Optional~Unfreeze selected layers with care | Does freezing a backbone guarantee suitable features for the new task? | No.
Evaluate useful errors, classes, groups and probability quality. A single average can hide important failures. Final-test evaluation checks the locked procedure; changing validation displays remain development evidence. | Counts~Which cases were missed?;Calibration~Do probabilities match frequencies?;Slices~Where does performance differ? | May the final evaluation select the most flattering subgroup? | No.
Deployment needs the input rules, preparation, network, output mapping and action rule. Weights alone are not enough. Changed feature order, units, shape or evaluation mode can break a correct model. | Contract~Shape, order, units, allowed values;Prediction~Saved transform plus model;Monitoring~Inputs, outcomes, and operational performance | Does detected input drift alone prove the model is wrong? | No. It prompts investigation of performance and context.
