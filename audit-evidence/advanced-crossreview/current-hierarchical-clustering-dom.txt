    - generic: Stage 02 · Understand
    - heading "Measuring \"Close\"" [level=2]: Measuring "Close"
    - paragraph: Before we can group anything, we must define what "similar" means. The most common way is to measure the physical distance between data points.
    - paragraph:
      - text: We use the
      - strong: Euclidean distance
      - text: (the straight-line distance) to find out who is closest to whom. It is common, but not the only option; Manhattan, cosine, or domain-specific distances can make more sense for different data.
    - text: d(A, B) = √[ (x₂ - x₁)² + (y₂ - y₁)² ]
    - paragraph: By measuring the distance between every single pair of points, we build a "distance matrix"—a lookup table for similarity.
    - paragraph:
      - strong: "Takeaway:"
      - text: Clustering depends on your distance choice, because "close" is a design decision.

    - generic: Stage 04 · Build
    - paragraph:

      - text: Ward linkage is tied to Euclidean geometry and increases in within-cluster variance. Single linkage can chain; complete linkage favours compact groups; average linkage is a compromise. Dendrogram heights do not represent the same quantity for every method.

    - generic: Complete the picture
    - heading "Scaling and cluster stability" [level=3]
    - paragraph: Standardise features when their units differ, unless those units deliberately encode importance. Otherwise one large-scale variable can dominate every merge.
    - paragraph: Dendrogram branches are descriptive, not automatically “true” clusters. Compare linkage choices, inspect stability, and use domain knowledge when selecting a cut height.
    - generic: Key takeaways
    - list:
      - listitem: The dendrogram records the order and distance of merges.
      - listitem: Linkage choice changes the geometry of the resulting groups.
      - listitem: Scaling and cut height must be justified, not selected because the picture looks pleasant.
