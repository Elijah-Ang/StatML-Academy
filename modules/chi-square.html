The Chi-Square Test of Independence

A formal method for determining whether categorical variables are truly independent, or if a meaningful pattern exists within the counts.

Scroll to begin ↓
Stage 01

The Raw Counts

The Chi-Square test evaluates categorical data—data placed into distinct bins or groups without an inherent numeric value.

Imagine we survey 50 students to see if there is a relationship between two categorical variables: Club Choice (Art vs. Sports) and Favorite Snack (Apples vs. Chips).

Rule of thumb: Chi-Square works with frequencies (counts), not averages. Every individual belongs to exactly one cell in our table.
Stage 02

Hypotheses & Marginals

Are club choice and favorite snack related, or are they completely independent? We formalize this question using hypotheses.

H₀
Null Hypothesis: Club choice and favorite snack are independent. (No relationship).
H₁
Alternative Hypothesis: Club choice and favorite snack are not independent. (There is a relationship).

To test this, we must first look at the margins. The row totals and column totals tell us the overall preferences of the group, ignoring the split between the variables.

Stage 03

Expected Counts

If the Null Hypothesis is perfectly true, the counts inside the table should be directly proportional to the totals on the margins. This gives us our Expected Counts—the baseline of what we would expect if there were no relationship at all between club choice and snack preference.

To find the expected count for any cell in our table, we use this simple formula:

Expected Count = (Row Total × Column Total) ÷ Grand Total

In standard algebraic shorthand, it is written as:

E = (Row Total × Column Total) / Grand Total
Step-by-Step Calculation: Art & Apple

Let's calculate the expected value for the first cell (Art students who prefer Apples):

Row Total (Art) = 30
Column Total (Apple) = 25
Grand Total = 50

E = (30 × 25) / 50 = 750 / 50 = 15

Why does this work?

Out of 50 total surveyed students, 25 like apples. That is exactly half (50%) of all students. If snack preference and club choice are completely unrelated (independent), then apples should be spread proportionally across both clubs.

Since 50% of the entire sample likes apples, we expect 50% of each club to like apples. Art has 30 students, and half of 30 is 15.

"If everything were random, about half of the Art students should like apples."

Calculating the Remaining Cells

We apply the exact same formula to the other three cells:

  • Art & Chips: E = (30 × 25) / 50 = 15
  • Sports & Apple: E = (20 × 25) / 50 = 10
  • Sports & Chips: E = (20 × 25) / 50 = 10
What is really happening?

The total number of apples is 25. If independent, we split these apples across the rows based on the size of each club:

  • Art has 30 out of 50 students (60% of the sample). Therefore, Art gets 60% of the expected apples: 60% of 25 = 15.
  • Sports has 20 out of 50 students (40% of the sample). Therefore, Sports gets 40% of the expected apples: 40% of 25 = 10.

The same logic applies to the chips category, which also has a column total of 25.

Expected count means: how much this row deserves × how much this column deserves.
Warning: Expected counts are not guesses from our opinion. They are calculated from the table totals under the assumption that the variables are independent.

Stage 04

Residuals & Contributions

The core intuition of Chi-Square is to compare what we Observed (O) with what we Expected (E). We square the residuals and divide by the expected count to scale the difference.

χ² = Σ [ (O - E)² / E ]
Worked Example: Art & Apple contribution

Let's calculate the individual score contribution for Art students who prefer Apples:

Observed (O) = 20
Expected (E) = 15
Residual (O - E) = 20 - 15 = 5
Square the residual: 5² = 25
Divide by expected: 25 ÷ 15 = 1.67

This cell contributes exactly 1.67 points to the overall Chi-Square statistic. Bigger contributions mean that cell is more responsible for the overall relationship.

Interactive Matrix: Hover over any cell in the table to inspect its Observed, Expected, Residual, and individual χ² contribution.

We do this for every cell and add them all together to get our final test statistic. (Note: visual contributions are shown rounded to two decimals, but the true sum is roughly 8.33).

Stage 05

The Reference Distribution

To evaluate our test statistic of 8.33, we map it onto a Chi-Square distribution. The curve's exact shape is determined by the Degrees of Freedom (df).

df = (Rows - 1) × (Cols - 1) = 1

If our statistic lands past the critical boundary (here, 3.84 for α = 0.05), the deviation is too large to be attributed to random chance.

Stage 06

Reading the Output

Statistical software synthesizes these steps into a standard output summary. Here is how to read the final result:

Analysis Output
Pearson's Chi-squared test
X-squared 8.33
df 1
p-value 0.0039
alpha (α) 0.05
Decision Reject H₀
Effect Size (Phi) 0.41 (Moderate)
✓ Expected counts check All expected counts ≥ 5
Understanding our p-value: The p-value is 0.0039. This means: if club choice and snack preference were truly unrelated, getting a pattern this strong or stronger would be very unlikely. So, we have strong evidence against the null hypothesis.
⚠️ Important: The p-value is not the probability that the null hypothesis is true. It is the probability of seeing a result this extreme or more extreme, assuming the null hypothesis were true.

Because the p-value (0.0039) is much smaller than our alpha threshold (0.05), we officially reject the null hypothesis. The Phi value of 0.41 confirms this is a moderate relationship.

What kind of relationship is it?

The p-value tells us: “Something is going on.” (The variables are associated).
The residuals tell us: “What exactly is going on.” (The direction and pattern of the association).

Group Apple Chips
Art +5 -5
Sports -5 +5

Let's break this down in human language:

  • Art students: The Art & Apple residual is +5 (more Art students liked apples than expected) and the Art & Chips residual is -5 (fewer Art students liked chips than expected). Therefore, Art students lean toward apples.
  • Sports students: The Sports & Apple residual is -5 (fewer Sports students liked apples than expected) and the Sports & Chips residual is +5 (more Sports students liked chips than expected). Therefore, Sports students lean toward chips.

Final Relationship Statement:

"There is a relationship between club choice and snack preference: Art students are more likely than expected to prefer apples, while Sports students are more likely than expected to prefer chips."

Caution: This is an association, not causation. We cannot say that joining Art causes students to like apples, or that joining Sports causes students to like chips.

Stage 07

Assumptions & Caveats

Before closing the book on this relationship, we must acknowledge the strict assumptions required for a valid Chi-Square test.

Required Conditions (Assumptions)

Before trusting any Chi-Square test, you must ensure your study meets these three non-negotiable assumptions:

1 Count Data (Frequencies)

Beginner meaning: You must use actual counts of people or items falling into categories, not averages or scores.

❌ Bad: using average happiness ratings or continuous test scores.
✓ Good: counting how many students chose apples or chips.
2 Independence of Observations

Beginner meaning: Each person should only appear once in the table. They cannot belong to multiple cells.

❌ Bad: the same student is counted under both Art and Sports.
✓ Good: each student belongs to one club category and one snack category.
3 Expected Counts are Large Enough

Beginner meaning: The expected numbers computed under independence should not be too tiny.

❌ Bad: many expected cells are 1 or 2 (which invalidates our p-value).
✓ Good: all expected counts in this example are at least 5 (our cells are 15, 15, 10, and 10).

Tiny expected counts can make the mathematical Chi-Square approximation highly unreliable. If this happens, researchers usually use Yates' continuity correction or switch to Fisher's Exact Test.

📖 The Whole Story in Plain English

Let's connect all the dots we've covered on this page into one simple, logical narrative:

  1. We counted students by club and snack: We started by putting students into categories based on two characteristics: their club (Art/Sports) and snack preference (Apples/Chips).
  2. We assumed independence: We asked: "What would this table look like if club and snack preferences had absolutely nothing to do with each other?"
  3. That gave us expected counts: Answering that question mathematically gave us our expected baseline counts (15, 15, 10, 10).
  4. We compared observed counts to expected counts: We looked at how far our actual observed numbers (20, 10, 5, 15) fell from that independent baseline.
  5. The differences were large enough: We squared and scaled these discrepancies, giving us a final test statistic of χ² = 8.33.
  6. The p-value was small, so we rejected independence: A discrepancy at least this large would be unusual under independence (p = 0.0039), assuming the test conditions hold. The data provide evidence against independence; Cramér’s V and residuals describe magnitude and contributing cells.
  7. The residuals showed the pattern: Looking closely at our cells, we saw the exact direction of this pattern—Art students lean apples, Sports students lean chips.
Synthesis

We have strong statistical evidence that club choice and snack preference are connected. However, Chi-Square demonstrates association, not causation. We cannot declare that joining the Art club causes students to eat apples.
