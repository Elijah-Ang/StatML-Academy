Why this works · a little deeper

A metric is a summary of predictions under a particular outcome definition and evaluation sample. It does not choose the application’s objective for you. Define the population, prediction time, available action, acceptable workload and cost of error. This lesson uses 1,000 fixed illustrative validation scores, of which 100 are positive. They are a constructed teaching dataset, not the performance of a medical product. No clinical decision should be inferred from the example.

Quick check · Why can two applications prefer different thresholds for the same model?

They may have different error costs, review capacity, prevalence or action constraints.

Next: Choose the denominator ↓
02Choose the denominator

Whose success rate are we measuring?

Accuracy looks at everyone. Precision looks only at alerts. Recall looks only at actual positives. The denominator changes the question, so the same predictions can produce very different percentages.

MetricNumerator / denominator
Accuracy(TP + TN) / all cases
PrecisionTP / (TP + FP)
RecallTP / (TP + FN)
SpecificityTN / (TN + FP)
F12TP / (2TP + FP + FN)
↳
Try it

Choose Precision or Recall in the visual. The matrix highlights the cases in its denominator.

Why this works · a little deeper

A true positive is a positive case correctly alerted; a true negative is a negative case correctly left unflagged. F1 is the harmonic mean of precision and recall when defined. It ignores true negatives and does not encode every practical cost. If no cases are predicted positive, precision has denominator zero and is undefined; showing 0% would conceal that distinction. Regression uses different quantities: MAE = mean |y − ŷ| and RMSE = √mean(y − ŷ)². Both are in outcome units; RMSE emphasizes larger misses. MAPE can fail near zero outcomes.

Quick check · For residuals 1 and 3, what are MAE and RMSE?

MAE = (1 + 3)/2 = 2. RMSE = √((1² + 3²)/2) = √5 ≈ 2.24. Larger misses receive more weight in RMSE.

Why this works · a little deeper

The counts reconcile: TP + FN = 100 positives, FP + TN = 900 negatives, and all four cells total 1,000. Precision is 80/170 ≈ 47.1%, so 90/170 ≈ 52.9% of alerts are false. Accuracy is 890/1,000 = 89%, yet predicting every case negative would achieve 90% accuracy and zero recall. That baseline illustrates why accuracy alone is not enough. None of these percentages captures uncertainty from sampling or label error.

Quick check · Which denominator answers “how many of the alerts were right”?

TP + FP, the total alerts. That gives precision, not recall.

Next: Separate ranking from decisions ↓
04Separate ranking from decisions

What does a threshold change?

The model’s scores stay fixed. A threshold converts them into yes/no decisions. A ROC curve compares recall with false-positive rate across thresholds; a precision–recall curve focuses on positive predictions.

QuestionUseful view
Can scores rank positives above negatives?ROC curve and AUC
How reliable are alerts as recall changes?Precision–recall curve
What happens at this one threshold?Confusion matrix and workload
↳
Try it

Compare ROC above with precision–recall below. Move the threshold and follow both operating points.

Why this works · a little deeper

ROC-AUC is the probability that a randomly chosen positive ranks above a randomly chosen negative, counting ties appropriately. It summarizes all operating regions, some of which may be irrelevant to the actual decision. Precision depends on prevalence, so PR curves help reveal the burden of false positives in rare-class problems. Neither ranking metric establishes calibration. These curves are calculated from the same fixed labeled scores as the matrix, at every distinct score threshold. With zero alerts, precision is undefined; the PR operating point is omitted rather than fabricated.

Quick check · If every score is changed by a strictly increasing transformation, must the ranking change?

No. Ranking can stay identical while the numerical probabilities and their calibration change.

Next: Assumptions and failure modes ↓
06Assumptions and failure modes

When is a metric answering the wrong question?

A precise calculation can still describe an unrepresentative sample. Check where the data came from, whether labels are trustworthy and whether the split matches how the model will be used.

Assumptions

Independent held-out predictions; a deployment-relevant sample; valid labels; the same outcome and decision definitions.

When the method fails

Leakage, distribution shift, misleading averages, tiny subgroups, unequal error costs and metric shopping.

↳
Try it

Raise the threshold to 1.00. See the high-accuracy all-negative baseline and its zero recall.

Why this works · a little deeper

Sensitivity and specificity may remain stable under a pure prior-probability shift, but precision changes with prevalence. If positive prevalence falls, false positives can dominate alerts even at a modest false-positive rate. Real distribution shifts can also change the class-conditional score distributions, so do not assume only prevalence changed. Report relevant subgroup performance with uncertainty rather than hiding it in an aggregate. Repeated patients require grouped evaluation; forecasts require time-respecting evaluation.

Quick check · Why can 99% accuracy be useless with 1% positives?

An all-negative rule attains 99% accuracy while missing every positive case.

Next: Use an honest workflow ↓
07Use an honest workflow

When do we look at the final test?

Define the goal and primary metric first. Develop and choose thresholds with validation data. Lock the full pipeline. Only then evaluate once on the protected test set.

  1. Define the decision and costs.
  2. Choose metrics and a relevant baseline.
  3. Fit and tune within development data.
  4. Lock preprocessing, model and threshold.
  5. Report final test estimates with uncertainty.
↳
Try it

Return to a threshold you can justify. State its trade-off before reading the scores.

Why this works · a little deeper

The pipeline includes imputation, scaling, feature selection, calibration and threshold selection—not just the final estimator. Cross-validation must repeat every learned step inside each training fold. Bootstrap intervals can describe sampling variability when their resampling unit matches independence; cluster or temporal dependence needs an appropriate resampling design. A final test is not a resource to repeatedly inspect and then retune. Our interactive scores remain validation examples throughout; this lesson does not pretend that trying a threshold constitutes a final test.

Quick check · What belongs on the final test set?

One evaluation of the locked procedure. Threshold searches and feature choices belong in development.

Next: Report probability quality too ↓
08Report probability quality too

Does “70%” behave like seventy out of a hundred?

Calibration compares predicted risks with observed frequencies. Brier score and log loss evaluate probability predictions. A good ranking does not guarantee honest probabilities.

Probability scoreSame fixed predictions
Brier: mean (p − y)²0.1371
Log loss: −mean log P(actual class)0.4530

What to report: definition, threshold, prevalence, baseline, sample size, uncertainty, costs and relevant subgroup results.

↳
Try it

Open the calibration view. Compare each bin’s average prediction with its observed positive fraction.

Why this works · a little deeper

Brier score is sensitive to both calibration and discrimination; it is not a pure measure of calibration. Log loss strongly penalizes confident mistakes. A reliability diagram bins predictions and compares mean predicted probability with observed frequency, but small bins are noisy and bin choices matter. The visual uses five fixed-width bins and shows their counts. A perfectly calibrated forecast can still discriminate poorly, such as always predicting the population prevalence. Recalibration is a learned transformation: fit it using development data, not the final test.

Quick check · Does a low Brier score prove every subgroup is calibrated?

No. It summarizes probability error overall. Inspect calibration, subgroup evidence and uncertainty separately.

Next: Check the reasoning ↓
09Check the reasoning

Can you explain the result without hiding behind a score?

A defensible evaluation connects the population, predictions, decision and consequences. Practice the five checks below, then explain your chosen threshold in a sentence.

“At threshold ___, we catch ___ of the positives, send ___ alerts, and accept ___ false alarms under these stated costs.”

Missed positive cases are especially costly. Which rate directly measures how many positives we catch?

Answer and explanation

Recall. Recall divides true positives by all actual positives. Still report false positives and workload.

No predictions are positive. What is precision?

Answer and explanation

Undefined: its denominator is zero. TP + FP is zero. State the undefined value and show that there were no alerts.

What does Brier score evaluate?

Answer and explanation

Squared probability error. It averages (p − y)² and reflects calibration and discrimination; it is not a pure calibration measure.

Where should a decision threshold be chosen?

Answer and explanation

Validation or out-of-fold predictions. Keep the final test independent of model and decision-rule selection.

What should accompany a point estimate?

Answer and explanation

A relevant baseline and uncertainty. Describe the sample, assumptions and uncertainty so the comparison can be interpreted.

Why this works · a little deeper

Keep the full path visible: trustworthy labels and split → fixed predictions → a prespecified metric and baseline → a validation choice → one final evaluation with uncertainty. Use the simplest quantity that answers the decision question while reporting enough complementary evidence to expose its blind spots. Neither accuracy nor F1 nor AUC can replace that chain of reasoning. Do not select whichever score happens to flatter a preferred model after seeing the results.

Quick check · What is missing from a report that says only “Accuracy = 89%”?

The sample, prevalence, baseline, confusion counts or relevant error rates, threshold, decision costs, uncertainty and how the evaluation was kept independent.
