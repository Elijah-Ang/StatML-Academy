- article:
  - generic: Stage 10 · Understand
  - heading "Stopping Rules" [level=2]: Stopping Rules
  - paragraph:
    - text: To prevent overfitting, we enforce
    - strong: Stopping Rules
    - text: (also known as pre-pruning). We restrict depth and control minimum partition requirements before splits can occur.
  - generic: "Validation Metric:"
  - button "RSS (Error Sum)"
  - button "MAE (Mean Error)"
  - button "RMSE (Typical Error)"
  - generic: Metric Decoder
  - generic: Error
  - text: Actual price − predicted price.
  - generic: RSS
  - text: Σ(actual − predicted)²
  - paragraph: Add up all squared errors. Excellent for comparing model error, though numbers become quite large due to squaring.
  - generic: MAE
  - text: average(|actual − predicted|)
  - paragraph: Average absolute mistake. Easiest to explain because it matches the original unit ($k).
  - generic: RMSE
  - text: √average((actual − predicted)²)
  - paragraph: Square root of average squared error. Expressed in original units ($k), but heavily punishes larger individual mistakes.
  - strong: "Example:"
  - text: If MAE = $30.0k, the model is off by about $30,000 on average. If RMSE = $33.0k, the typical error is about $33,000, with bigger mistakes punished more strongly.
  - generic: Why compare Train vs Validation?
  - text: •
  - strong: Training Error
  - text: tells us how well the tree fits the data it learned from.
  - text: •
  - strong: Validation Error
  - text: tells us how well it performs on unseen, hidden houses.
  - generic: • A deep tree can drive training error down to zero by memorizing noise, but will perform poorly on validation data.
  - text: •
  - strong: "Rule of thumb:"
  - text: Always choose settings that minimize validation error. If two trees perform similarly, choose the simpler one!

  - list:

- article:
  - generic: Stage 15 · Reflect
  - 'heading "Reality Check: Limitations" [level=2]': "Reality Check: Limitations"
  - paragraph: While extremely powerful and explainable, single regression trees have key engineering limitations that you must look out for in practice.
  - text: Poor Extrapolation
  - generic: Trend Fail
  - paragraph: Trees predict flat averages. If a house size is larger than any seen in training (e.g., 220 m²), the tree cannot continue a trend upward; it flatlines at the average of the largest leaf.
  - text: Jagged Step Predictions
  - generic: Staircase
  - paragraph: Real-world factors often rise smoothly. Trees build staircases with sudden step jumps, struggling to cleanly represent smooth curves without huge depths.
  - text: High Instability (Variance)
  - generic: High Variance
  - paragraph: A tiny shift in data (e.g., swapping one point's price) can completely re-arrange the root split boundary, producing a highly unstable prediction layout.
  - text: Axis-Aligned Splits
  - generic: Orthogonal
  - paragraph: Basic trees split one feature at a time at right angles, making it difficult to capture diagonal correlations (like price dropping when size decreases and age increases simultaneously).
  - text: Easy to Overfit
  - generic: High Complexity
  - paragraph: Without strict stopping rules or pruning, deep trees will easily grow until they isolate every single point, memorizing individual training points instead of discovering generalizable patterns.
  - strong: "Interactive Demo:"
  - text: Click any card above to see a live visual simulation of that limitation on the right canvas.

  - generic: Stage 16 · Reflect
  - heading "Synthesis" [level=2]: Synthesis
  - paragraph: "Let's review the final sequence to build and use a regression tree:"
  - generic: "1."
  - text: Put all training data in the
  - strong: Root Node
  - text: .
  - generic: "2."
  - generic: Test possible splits (greedy thresholds) across all available features.
  - generic: "3."
  - text: Pick the feature/split that results in the
  - strong: lowest RSS
  - text: .
  - generic: "4."
  - generic: Repeat the process recursively inside each new branch.
  - generic: "5."
  - generic: Use validation error to choose stopping rules or pruning, instead of trusting training error alone.
  - generic: "6."
  - text: Each final leaf predicts the
  - strong: average target value
  - text: inside that leaf.
  - paragraph:
    - text: While a single regression tree can be unstable on its own, this recursive logic is the fundamental building block for advanced modern algorithms like
    - strong: Random Forests
    - text: and
    - strong: Gradient Boosting
    - text: .
  - strong: "Ensemble Learning:"
  - text: Ensembles improve single trees by combining hundreds of them, smoothing out jagged steps and yielding highly stable predictions.
  - generic: Complete the picture
  - heading "Extrapolation and uncertainty" [level=3]
  - paragraph: A regression tree cannot naturally extrapolate a trend beyond the training range. New extreme values usually inherit the prediction of the outermost leaf.
  - paragraph: Single-tree predictions can be unstable. Ensembles such as random forests or gradient boosting reduce variance, while conformal or bootstrap methods can add uncertainty estimates when required.
  - generic: Key takeaways
  - list:
    - listitem: Splits are chosen to reduce within-node squared error.
    - listitem: Pruning trades a little training fit for better generalisation.
    - listitem: Trees capture interactions and nonlinearities but predict in steps and extrapolate poorly.
