01The whole idea: learn patterns from examples

What does learning from examples mean?

A neural network combines small calculations. It multiplies inputs by weights, adds starting values, and applies activation functions. Training changes the weights and starting values to reduce prediction loss. This lab uses a small network whose calculations can be inspected.

Next: A network is layers of tiny calculators ↓
04A network is layers of tiny calculators

What does a layer compute?

Each neuron multiplies its inputs by weights, adds a bias, then applies an activation. A bias is a starting value. Different neurons can react to different combinations of the same inputs.

Follow the ideaRead it in plain language
Inputsx₁ and x₂
Preactivationw₁x₁ + w₂x₂ + b
Outputactivation(preactivation)

Try it

Change an input or the first weight. Follow the two products and bias into the weighted sum, then through the activation curve.

Why this works · a little deeper

For neuron j, z_j=w₁j x₁+w₂j x₂+b_j. z is the weighted sum and b the bias. Activation transforms z into the neuron's output. The responses are numerical features; they are not guaranteed to represent human-named ideas such as sleep or effort. More units can add capacity, computation and overfitting risk.

05One feature has a different weight into every hidden neuron

Why does each connection have its own weight?

Every connection has its own weight. A weight is the multiplier on that connection. A bias shifts the neuron's sum. Change one weight and follow the effect through the later calculations.

Why this works · a little deeper

There is no one universal weight for an input. It can connect to several neurons with different multipliers. A negative weight can lower a sum for a positive input. A negative input times a negative weight raises it. Follow the actual products and totals. A weight alone rarely gives a complete human explanation of a distributed network.

Next: Non-linearity lets stacked layers learn bends ↓
06Non-linearity lets stacked layers learn bends

Why are nonlinear activations necessary?

An activation can bend the response. Without a bend between layers, stacked weighted sums collapse into one weighted-sum rule. Bending lets the network represent more complex relationships than one straight rule.

A small example · separate from the live dataset
Follow the ideaRead it in plain language
ReLUmax(0, z)
SigmoidMaps to (0, 1)
TanhMaps to (−1, 1)

Your notebook: Open the visual to inspect the current calculation.

↳
Try it

Choose an activation and compare its bend with the decision boundary below. Changing activation starts a fresh run.

Why this works · a little deeper

ReLU(z)=max(0,z): it passes positive values and sets negative ones to zero. Tanh bends into a range from −1 to 1. Sigmoid maps a raw output to a 0–1 probability for a binary task. Softmax converts several class scores into probabilities summing to 1. A regression output can remain an unrestricted number. ReLU does not mean the entire network discards every kind of negative evidence; other units can represent it.

Next: Loss measures how wrong the prediction was ↓
08Loss measures how wrong the prediction was

What does loss measure?

Loss turns the quality of a prediction into a number to minimize. Binary cross-entropy gives a large penalty to a very confident wrong probability. A correct label alone does not tell the whole probability story.

A small example · separate from the live dataset
Follow the ideaRead it in plain language
True label1
p = 0.8Loss −log(0.8) ≈ 0.223
p = 0.2Loss −log(0.2) ≈ 1.609

Your notebook: Open the visual to inspect the current calculation.

↳
Try it

Change the inspected true label while retaining the same prediction. Follow the point on the corresponding cross-entropy curve.

Why this works · a little deeper

For binary targets, loss is −[y log(p)+(1−y)log(1−p)]. If y=1, assigning tiny p produces a large penalty. The training objective averages appropriate row losses, possibly adding a penalty. Loss measures a chosen objective; it is not automatically a measure of practical harm, calibration or fairness.

Quick check · Is accuracy smooth enough to be the usual gradient-training objective?

No. Cross-entropy supplies useful differentiable feedback.

Next: Backpropagation computes gradients—only gradients ↓
09Backpropagation computes gradients—only gradients

What does backpropagation compute?

Backpropagation calculates gradients: how a small change in each weight or bias would change loss. It works backwards through the calculation. It does not itself choose the step size or update the weights.

A small example · separate from the live dataset
Follow the ideaRead it in plain language
Output error signalp − y for sigmoid plus cross-entropy
Hidden gradientChain of local derivatives
ResultOne derivative per parameter

Your notebook: Open the visual to inspect the current calculation.

↳
Try it

Inspect the measured loss surface and gradient components. The downhill arrow is opposite the gradient; computing it alone has not updated weights.

Why this works · a little deeper

A gradient is a collection of local slopes. The chain rule connects how one operation changes the next, all the way to loss. Backpropagation computes those slopes using the same parameter snapshot. The gradient points locally uphill; a downhill step goes in the opposite direction. The visible landscape is a two-weight slice with other parameters fixed, not the full high-dimensional surface.

Quick check · Must the weights change while gradients are being calculated?

No.

Next: The optimizer takes iterative, local steps ↓
10The optimizer takes iterative, local steps

What does the optimizer change?

Gradient descent updates a parameter by subtracting learning rate times its gradient. The learning rate is the step size. A step that is too large can make the loss worse instead of better.

A small example · separate from the live dataset
Follow the ideaRead it in plain language

Gradient0.3
Learning rate 0.1New weight 0.37

↳
Try it

Choose a learning rate, predict the change in loss, then take one two-weight step. Rewind to recover the exact previous parameters.

Why this works · a little deeper

New weight = old weight − rate×gradient. If the gradient is +2 and the rate .1, subtract .2. This is a local step, not proof it reaches the global best fit. Large steps may overshoot. The lab's two-weight step and full-network training updates are separate operations with their scope stated. Rewind restores the previous saved parameters.

Quick check · What happens with a negative gradient?

Subtracting it increases the parameter.

11One update has a strict causal order

Why must operations happen in this order?

Use one unchanged weight snapshot for the forward pass, loss and gradients. Only then apply the update. Mixing old and newly updated weights changes the intended calculation.

Why this works · a little deeper

A batch is the examples used for one update. An iteration is one update; an epoch is one full pass through the training data. For 1,000 rows and batch size 100, a simple full-pass setup uses 10 iterations per epoch. The lab's ordered cycle makes calculation and update distinct, rather than animating weights during gradient computation.
